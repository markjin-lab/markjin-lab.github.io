A 1,350-cell experiment finds that faults calibrated to the public MCP ecosystem cut the task-completion score from 8.08 to 3.23 out of 10 (about a 60% drop), and that for the two gpt-oss backbones tested, a larger model did not buy higher fault tolerance [1]. ## Today's news through the Unified Systems View

For what the Unified Systems View is, see the [About page](/en/about/). All three papers in this issue can be read on arXiv; the arXiv entry for item 1 states Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (poster), while the entries for items 2 and 3 do not state publication or review status [1], [2], [3].

**Item 1: When tool-calling agents hit realistic protocol faults, does a larger model hold up better?**

Background: language-model agents increasingly rely on external tools exposed via the Model Context Protocol (MCP), yet current benchmarks often assume tools respond correctly and on time, leaving resilience under realistic failures untested [1]. (General explanation added by this site: MCP usually means an interface convention for calling external tools under a shared protocol; an agent here usually means a language-model system that can call tools and keep state across turns.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-23, “Tool-Calling LLM Agents Under Realistic MCP Faults: Scale Does Not Purchase Fault Tolerance”, six authors—Antonio Ken Iannillo, Joshua Segun Owotogbe, Roberto Natella, Francesco Avallone, Kristina Kudryavtseva and Indika Kumara—introduce MCP-Injector, a protocol-aware fault-injection middleware on the tool-call path, with a fault model calibrated to the observed incident distribution of the public MCP ecosystem, mined from 57,240 issue and pull-request threads across 1,774 open-source MCP repositories; the arXiv comment states Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (poster) [1].

Why it matters: in a 1,350-cell experiment crossing two backbone sizes (20B, 120B), three agent stacks and three fault-injection conditions, faults drawn from this permanent-heavy stress profile cut the task-completion score from 8.08 to 3.23 out of 10 (about a 60% drop) [1]. For the two gpt-oss backbones tested, scale does not purchase fault tolerance: the 120B retains no larger share of its fault-free score under faults (resilience-ratio model, p = 0.96) [1]. Agent stacks differ in how well they absorb faults; the authors suggest that framework-level error handling matters more than model scale [1].

Where it sticks: the abstract reports the overall drop under that fault profile, the resilience comparison for two gpt-oss backbones, and a qualitative claim about stack differences, without per-stack completion scores or corresponding figures for other protocols or backbone families [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, under other tool-protocol fault distributions or other backbone and agent-stack setups, still find that a larger backbone retains no larger share of its fault-free task-completion score, or that framework-level error handling still explains fault absorption better than simply scaling the model.

**Item 2: Can off-the-shelf models keep fine-grained spatiotemporal memory over long videos?**

Background: people process multimodal streams spanning hours to years and build fine-grained spatiotemporal memories that support long-horizon reasoning, while existing benchmarks lean toward short and medium contexts and most foundation models struggle to preserve fine-grained information over long horizons [2]. (General explanation added by this site: spatiotemporal memory here usually means a persistent representation of what happened where and when, used later for questions and prediction.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-23, “4DMemory: Benchmarking Long-Horizon Spatiotemporal Memory with Harness-Augmented Agents”, ten authors led by Yanhao Jia introduce the LongVideo dataset and 4DMemoryBench [2]. LongVideo comprises about 900 hours of video and more than 5,000 manually reviewed questions covering visual, textual and spatiotemporal reasoning across diverse context lengths [2]. They further develop the 4DMemory Harness, which integrates four complementary skills, 22+ specialized tool models, and a model-specific retrieval-augmented generation system [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a harness here usually means an execution frame around a model that schedules tools, retrieval and related steps.)

Why it matters: evaluating 13 leading models, the authors find that standalone foundation models struggle with long-horizon tasks, while the 4DMemory Harness improves all of them by over 50% [2]. Compared with a previous benchmark, the same models exhibit a performance drop of more than 10% on 4DMemoryBench [2].

Where it sticks: the abstract reports an over-50% gain relative to standalone use and a more-than-10% drop relative to a previous benchmark, without absolute per-model scores or per-skill or per-tool contribution figures [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other long-video corpora or other tool-orchestration setups, still find standalone foundation models clearly weaker than harnessed combinations, and whether the gap relative to shorter-context benchmarks remains common.

**Item 3: Can an agent harness spend fewer tokens without giving up success?**

Background: agent harnesses often invoke reasoning again after tool feedback, even when an earlier call has already fixed the next action [3].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-23, “HarnessMM: Recursive Self-Improvement for Harness Token Efficiency”, four authors—Yuhua Jiang, Chang Xu, Feifei Gao and Li Zhao—introduce HarnessMM (Harness Minus Minus), which improves harness token efficiency through guarded decision reuse and recursive self-improvement; a controller at the model–tool boundary reuses resolved decisions to replace later reasoning callbacks while actor weights remain fixed [3]. (General explanation added by this site: a token usually means the basic unit a model uses when reading or writing text, and it directly affects inference cost and latency.)

Why it matters: on HumanEval, HarnessMM preserves Codex success at 90.82% while reducing Tokens/task (k) from 66.72 to 47.02 (29.54% saving) [3]. For DeepSeek Harness, success rises from 71.43% to 85.71% while Tokens/task (k) fall from 96.28 to 11.62 (87.93% saving) [3]. The authors present guarded decision reuse as a practical way to reduce repeated reasoning in existing harnesses [3].

{{< industry-link item="3" industry="ai" side="cost" inference="a lower Tokens/task figure translates into lower inference cost for this kind of agent harness" >}}The source reports harness changes that lower Tokens/task while holding or raising success [3], which maps to inference cost for agent orchestration in this industry.{{< /industry-link >}}

Where it sticks: the abstract reports success and Tokens/task figures for HumanEval and DeepSeek Harness; a separate MBPP ablation reports 26.91% lower cost than single-use graphs and 19.52% lower cost than retained callbacks, but that contrast is a cost metric rather than Tokens/task, and it still gives no dollar or GPU-hour cost gap [3].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other coding benchmarks or other harness setups, still see guarded decision reuse cut Tokens/task without a clear sacrifice in success.
