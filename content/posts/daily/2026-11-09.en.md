**Item 1: How close are large language model agents to working as circuit-board routing engineers?**

Background: The abstract says automating printed circuit board (PCB) routing requires coordinating board-level connectivity decisions with geometric execution under strict design rules, and that although recent agents can interact with native routing tools, reliable routing on complex boards remains challenging [1]. (General explanation added by this site: routing means laying out the traces that carry electrical connections between components on a board.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "PCBRouteAgent: How Close Are LLM Agents to Becoming PCB Routing Engineers?", five authors—Yue Zhang, Jilei Sun, Liqiang Jing, Yijie Wei and Ruohan Yang—present PCBRouteAgent, a multimodal routing agent inspired by how human engineers coordinate global strategy with local execution: a Global Routing Planner combines board images with structured electrical and geometric information to specify connection topology, routing order, corridors and layer transitions; a Detailed Routing Executor realizes these guides through KiCad’s native routing engine with bounded local recovery; and unresolved failures return as structured feedback for targeted guide revisions while accepted routes are preserved [1].

Why it matters: On 247 synthetic and real-world boards from four PCBWorld-Bench test splits, it achieves clean-pass rates of 100%, 94.9%, 100% and 40% on D2, D3-A, D3-B and D3-C; on the three real-world subsets it beats the best evaluated baseline in clean-pass rate by 13.9, 36 and 10 percentage points [1].

Where it sticks: On the D3-C subset the clean-pass rate is only 40% [1]. The abstract gives no direct comparison with human engineers [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on more complex real boards or with other routing tools, can get large language model agents above half clean passes on the hardest tier.

**Item 2: When an AI answer claims to rest on sources, can users actually inspect them, and do they check?**

Background: The abstract says large language models often present synthesized answers without making their supporting evidence easy to inspect [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "Signals, Evidence, and Context: User Scrutiny in Real-World LLM Conversations", two authors—Ahana Biswas and Yu-Ru Lin—study how features of answers relate to users’ subsequent accepting, checking and challenging in 489,283 assistant–user response pairs from ShareChat and WildChat [2].

Why it matters: Broad attribution language appears in 31.9% of qualifying responses, compared with explicit URLs in 1.33%, revealing a gap between claims of grounding and preserved inspectable support [2]. Attribution, uncertainty and other response cues generally accompany both checking and challenging; only uncertainty shows a small, statistically distinguishable pooled association favoring checking [2].

Where it sticks: The abstract reports associations between answer features and user behavior [2]. Scrutiny is concentrated in some conversations, and its apparent turn-to-turn persistence becomes much smaller when conversation-level differences and response ordering are examined separately [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, in other conversation data or products, also find broad attribution language far outnumbering explicit links, and the same association between expressed uncertainty and user checking.
