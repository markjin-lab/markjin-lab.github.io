This issue presents three research leads: how to arrange the visual components of a model, how to compare models when users adapt, and what to examine when agents delegate tasks. All three papers were submitted to arXiv on 2026-11-12 (UTC). We introduce the questions in their abstracts without presenting them as verified research findings [1], [2], [3].

## Research lead one: separating the choices behind visual understanding

**Unbundling Multimodal Vision Encoding**, Ellis Brown and colleagues, arXiv:2611.17334v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [1].

The abstract asks how to separate three choices bundled into a vision encoder: pretraining, dedicated visual capacity, and placing that capacity before visual and linguistic information are combined [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a vision encoder turns an image into representations a subsequent model can process; multimodal systems handle different forms of information, such as images and text.)

The reason to read further is that this turns a single question about keeping or removing a vision encoder into several distinct comparisons. Our next questions for the full paper are whether models use comparable data and computing budgets, and how the authors distinguish visual question answering from language tasks. We have not checked the full text. We therefore do not report its performance, training-cost or response-time conclusions, or recommend a particular architecture.

## Research lead two: comparing models when users change their questions

**When Both Sides Adapt: The Challenges of Evaluating Interactive LLM Systems**, Hannah Li, arXiv:2611.17239v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [2].

The abstract considers users changing their prompting strategy, task type or task difficulty after seeing a model’s responses, and raises distinctions relevant to model comparisons [2]. (General explanation added by this site: offline evaluation commonly uses a prepared set of questions; live A/B testing exposes different users to different alternatives and compares observed behavior or outcomes.)

Our reading question is whether comparing two models on the same questions answers the same decision problem as comparing them on questions generated after users adapt to each model. That is a question for checking the full paper, not a verified claim that the rankings must reverse. We have not checked theorem assumptions, detailed study design or real-user data. We do not turn the abstract’s theoretical discussion into an empirical finding about a particular online product.

Short extract from the abstract:

> users are adaptive
>
> Translation: users adapt their behavior.

## Research lead three: keeping useful information after handing off a task

**Lost in Delegation: Studying the Effectiveness of Task Orchestration in Generative Search**, Tamara Stojanovska and colleagues, arXiv:2611.16526v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [3].

The abstract discusses delegating work to external tools or other models and asks when delegation is useful, including tasks involving information outside the orchestrating model’s knowledge [3]. (General explanation added by this site: an agent connects a model to tools for executing tasks; delegation hands a subtask to another actor and uses the information returned.)

Our next reading questions concern whether comparisons use the same models, tools and task conditions; how tasks are split around the model’s knowledge cutoff; and whether returned evidence is retained and used in the answer. We have not checked the full text. This issue makes no claim that multi-agent systems perform better or worse, reports no accuracy, cost or speed changes, and does not turn the lead into advice for a particular delegation workflow.

Short extract from the abstract:

> when and how delegation improves performance remains poorly understood
>
> Translation: understanding of when and how delegation improves performance remains limited.
