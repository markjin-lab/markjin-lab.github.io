A test planted 100 known errors into 10 open-access psychology papers and ran them through frontier models and two commercial AI review tools; the best single system caught 71 of the errors, the worst caught 30, and pooling every system’s output caught 93 [1]. Another study uses honeypot services to fingerprint AI agents carrying out cyberattacks, distinguishing among 15 candidate models with an average 90% top-1 accuracy [2].

**Item 1: When AI reviews a paper for errors, how many does it find, and what does it miss?**

Background: The author planted 100 known errors into 10 open-access psychology papers and then ran the modified papers through frontier models and two commercial AI review tools [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-09, "How Well Does AI Peer Review Work? A Benchmark Built from 100 Planted Errors" (entry states accepted at the non-archival workshop CogSec 2026), the author, Paul Litvak, builds a benchmark from this setup; the papers, errors, model outputs and full experiment log are public [1].

Why it matters: The best single system caught 71 of the 100 errors, while the worst caught 30; pooling every system’s output caught 93 [1]. Models are only partly correlated in the errors they find, which the abstract says makes ensembling a big lever for finding issues in papers [1]. Seven errors could not be caught by any system, and all seven were omissions: information deleted from a paper rather than mistakes inserted into it [1].

Where it sticks: The abstract states that false positives were not measured, and that how this error distribution compares with errors in real papers remains unknown [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, testing AI review in other fields or on real submissions, find single systems again catching around seven in ten errors, pooled systems above nine in ten, and omissions still the hardest to find.

**Item 2: When AI agents carry out cyberattacks, can defenders tell which model and which harness are behind them?**

Background: The abstract says the objective is to reliably infer information about an attacker’s configuration, such as the large language model version and harness, from telemetry [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a harness is the software around a model that calls tools and runs commands; telemetry means logs and similar data a system records automatically while running.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-09, "Say Shibboleth: Fingerprinting the LLMs and Harnesses Behind Agentic Attacks" (entry states v0.1), five authors—Dario Pasquini, Reworr, Giuseppe Ateniese, Manish Bhatt and Sergej Epp—introduce shibboleth traps, modular honeypot services that make informative interactions a condition for obtaining resources the attacking agent seeks, eliciting model declarations and characteristic writing without requiring a successful prompt injection; to fingerprint harnesses, they indirectly probe execution behaviors such as how a harness masks secrets in tool output, enforces command time limits and schedules tool calls [2].

Why it matters: Combined declarative and behavioral inference distinguishes among 15 candidate models with an average 90% top-1 accuracy, with behavioral classifiers evaluated on held-out harnesses or task families [2]. In a separate experiment across four harnesses, fixed probe rules identify the correct harness with 92% accuracy [2].

Where it sticks: The entry states v0.1 [2]. The abstract says that when agents are instructed to conceal their identity, their declarations become uninformative, but they still leave useful behavioral evidence [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on real attack telemetry or with more candidate models, can likewise identify the model and harness behind attacking agents with about 90% accuracy, especially when agents deliberately conceal their identity.
