**Item 1: When billing is per token, can a few-parameter formula estimate inference energy near one-third the error of today’s best fit models without per-model calibration?**

Background: Generative AI is often billed per token, yet tokens are not equally expensive; each token enters the KV cache and raises the cost of those that follow, so energy grows non-linearly along a sequence. The abstract says no analytical model states what a token costs, and the most accurate SOTA model is a fit, not a law: it absorbs architecture into fitted coefficients and needs per-LLM calibration [1]. (General explanation added by this site: prefill usually means the stage that processes the input before an answer is generated; decode usually means the later stage that generates the answer step by step; a KV cache usually means the key–value state kept for already processed input and output tokens so later steps can reuse it.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-02, "The SIMPLE Law for Energy Cost of LLM Inference" (entry states Submitted to Future Generation Computer Systems), five authors—Hiari Pizzini Cavagna, Andrea Proia, Daniele Cesarini, Zeynep Kiziltan and Andrea Bartolini—derive closed-form expressions for the computational and memory-access complexity of LLM inference from architectural hyperparameters, covering MHA and GQA, compare a family of analytical models of increasing granularity, and obtain SIMPLE (SIMPLE Inference Model to Predict LLMs Energy), a four-parameter law that separates prefill from decode and splits decode into KV-cache cost and next-token computation [1].

Why it matters: over traces of 13 dense LLMs from six families on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, it attains R² = 0.969 and 11.97% MAPE, 3.1–3.5× below the SOTA, without per-LLM calibration [1]. (In plain terms: R² measures how well a fit matches the data—higher is better; MAPE is mean absolute percentage error—lower is better.) The abstract also reports that more aggressive GQA KV grouping cuts per-token energy by up to 3× relative to standard MHA; that raising the reasoning-token budget pushes output length past an analytically derived energy minimum and drives super-linear energy growth, with Intelligence per Joule peaking at an intermediate budget and falling by up to 3.14× across a 4.0× reasoning-budget range; and that agentic serving raises energy per request by 13.0× relative to non-agentic ones, while prefix caching removes most of the prefill cost (−93%) but leaves the KV-cache term unaffected, so even a cached agentic request still costs 4.8× more [1].

**Item 2: Must MoE expert gradients stay in GPU memory until the optimizer step, or can peak memory fall if they are freed sooner?**

Background: Most parameters of a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model sit in its experts, and standard training keeps every expert gradient in GPU memory until the optimizer step. The abstract says this memory grows with the number and size of the experts as MoE models scale [2]. (General explanation added by this site: Mixture-of-Experts usually means a model that routes each input to a subset of expert subnetworks; peak memory usually means the highest GPU memory footprint during a training step.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-02, "Sonar-MoE: In-Backward Expert Updates for Memory-Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Training", ten authors—Avni Mittal, Avinash Anand, Ashutosh Kumar, Dikshant Kukreja, Kritarth Prasad, Chaitanya Lakhchaura, Rishitej Reddy Vyalla, Erik Cambria, Zhengkui Wang and Rajiv Ratn Shah—propose Sonar-MoE, which updates each expert tensor during the backward pass and frees its gradient right away, bringing optimizer-in-backward from dense models to batched MoE expert tensors so the GPU never holds more than one layer of expert gradients [2].

Why it matters: when fine-tuning OLMoE-1B-7B and Granite-3.1-3B-A800M in bf16 with two expert-kernel backends, Sonar-MoE lowers peak memory by 19% to 22% [2]. Its updates are bitwise identical to standard training without expert gradient clipping, and held-out loss stays within 0.003 of all baselines; each step also takes 17% to 23% less time than standard training with global-norm clipping [2].

**Item 3: Can the “how to infer” half of an agent system be searched across tasks the way model weights are trained, instead of being hand-written each time?**

Background: The abstract says a model is only half of an AI agent system; the other half is the inference strategy—the reasoning procedure that turns model calls into a response—and today that second half is written by hand, so experience from one strategy rarely carries to the next [3].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-02, "Strategist: Evolving Reasoning with Agents" (entry states NeurIPS 2026), four authors—Taiming Lu, Qi Xu, Zhigang Hua and Zhuang Liu—introduce Strategist, a meta-agent that evolves inference strategies across tasks; each strategy is a blueprint of executable code that other strategies can reuse, and the blueprints form a library; given a task and a small development set, it retrieves related blueprints, writes candidates, tests them on real samples, revises them from the results, and adds every candidate to the library so later tasks build on earlier discoveries [3].

Why it matters: on 30 benchmarks, Strategist beats the strongest baseline on 24 and matches its average accuracy (74.7% vs 74.0%) at 39% lower inference cost [3]. The abstract also says its strategies transfer to unseen tasks and other models without further search [3].
