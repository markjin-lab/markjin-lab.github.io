**Item 1: If an assistant confidently says something false, will people go along?**

Background: research on sycophancy asks whether large language models bend towards what their users want to hear [1]. (General explanation added by this site: sycophancy usually means a model agreeing with a user's preferences or expectations in order to please them, even when that is not correct.) This paper asks the reverse: when a model is confident, consistent, and wrong, do users bend towards it [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-22, “Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration”, three authors, Deepthika S, Pawni Yadav and B. Sankar, run a within-subjects experiment in which 32 participants learn up to five science topics by chatting with five assistants built on the same model (GPT-5-mini) [1]. Unknown to participants, each assistant's briefing had 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, or 100% of its facts replaced by plausible falsehoods, and every assistant spoke with full confidence [1]. A surprise quiz after each topic offered the correct fact, the planted falsehood, and two distractors, with a confidence rating for every answer [1].

Why it matters: across 104 quizzes and 1,040 answers, scores fell from 7.4 to 2.9 out of 10, and the planted option was chosen on 60% of falsified items against 11% of correctly taught ones (odds ratio 12.5) [1]. Transcript coding showed that whenever the assistant stated a falsehood, it was adopted about 65% of the time at every falsification level [1]. Absorbed falsehoods were held with high confidence (8.4/10); the modeled confidence advantage of right over wrong answers vanished, and the discrimination of confidence for correctness fell from an AUROC of 0.70 to chance, while post-task trust ratings barely moved [1]. (General explanation added by this site: AUROC usually means the area under a receiver-operating-characteristic curve used to score how well a classifier separates correct from incorrect outcomes.) The assistant itself was almost never sycophantic and never conceded under pushback [1]. The authors introduce a baseline-corrected Human Deference Index and argue that trustworthiness is a property of the human–AI pair, that sycophancy evaluations should measure both directions, and that learning interfaces must make reliability legible claim by claim [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other models or topics, using a setup in which assistants state planted falsehoods with full confidence, still see adoption near about two-thirds when a falsehood is stated, and a collapse of confidence discrimination for correctness toward chance.

**Item 2: When an AI proposes a decision and a confidence score, should an expert be called in?**

Background: online monitoring of an AI system that produces a sequence of decisions asks, at each round, whether to accept a proposed decision automatically or consult an expert such as a human auditor [2]. The system outputs a proposed decision together with a confidence score; the decision and the score need not come from the same model—for example, one agent may propose while a separate verifier scores correctness or safety [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-22, “Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing”, four authors, Isaac Gibbs, Elise L. Han, Ryan J. Tibshirani and Tijana Zrnic, formulate this task as an online constrained optimization problem: minimize the frequency of expert auditing while keeping the fraction of AI errors that go unaudited below a user-defined threshold [2]. They derive a family of algorithms with provable error-control guarantees with no assumptions, including a method that asymptotically attains the best achievable auditing rate for both adversarial and i.i.d. data streams [2].

Why it matters: experiments on screening harmful requests to large language models, qualitative text annotation, and data labeling show that the methods achieve valid error control in practice while substantially reducing the need for human auditing compared with baselines [2].

**Item 3: N-gram embedding tables use a lot of memory; can low-bit storage cut that cost?**

Background: many recent open-weight language models use n-gram embedding tables to improve performance; these tables store learned representations of short token sequences, such as pairs or triples of consecutive tokens, which the model retrieves during processing [3]. (General explanation added by this site: an n-gram usually means a short sequence of consecutive tokens, and an embedding table usually means a parameter table that maps discrete symbols to vector representations the model can look up.) The tables can require substantial memory: the authors note that DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, for example, allocates 196 billion parameters to its Engram n-gram memory [3]. They therefore investigate whether this memory cost can be reduced while preserving the performance improvement the tables provide [3].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-22, “Low-Bit N-Gram Embeddings for Language Models”, two authors, Stephen Chung and Wenyu Du, propose storing n-gram embeddings at low precision and systematically investigate different quantization methods and bit allocations across tables [3]. (General explanation added by this site: quantization usually means approximating higher-precision parameters with a lower-bit numeric format.)

Why it matters: across nanochat-, Llama-, and GPT-2-family models trained from scratch, four-bit tables save approximately 75% of table memory while preserving more than 99% of the validation-loss improvement from adding the tables [3]. Using fewer bits leads to a larger drop in performance, although a short fine-tuning phase recovers a significant part of this loss [3]. Reinvesting the saved memory in larger tables can improve performance beyond that of the original full-precision tables while still using less memory [3]. On large existing models DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, low-bit tables save 47–55% of table memory, with perplexity increases of less than 1.2% and largely preserved downstream task performance [3].
