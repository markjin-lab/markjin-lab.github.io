Sable can prove that a large language model’s output was computed, bit for bit, by a committed integer-quantized model; on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, it proves all 64 layers of Qwen2.5-32B for 4,096 tokens in 92.5 seconds, and a proof costs 6.4 cents per 4,096-token request at public GPU prices [1]. Another study of more than 1.26 million blind votes on Chatbot Arena finds that, for the same pair of fixed-version models, the share of “both are bad” votes rises by 1.71 percentage points a month, while the ranking shows no trend [2].

## Today’s news through the Unified Systems View

For what the Unified Systems View is, see the [About page](/en/about/). All three papers in this issue can be read on arXiv; the arXiv entry for item 2 states working paper, and the entries for items 1 and 3 do not state publication or review status [1], [2], [3].

**Item 1: Can a single GPU produce a bit-exact proof for one full large language model inference, at a cost measured in cents?**

Background: Sable proves the statement that a committed, integer-quantized transformer produced a given token sequence from a given prompt, bit for bit; to do so it fixes an integer function with no floating-point step, in which every rounding, normalization, softmax and sampling decision is determined by its inputs [1]. (General explanation added by this site: integer quantization means representing a model’s parameters and intermediate computations as integers; “committed” means a cryptographic fingerprint of the model weights is fixed in advance, so the weights cannot be swapped afterwards.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-10, "Sable: A Small-Field Sumcheck Argument for Bit-Exact Inference of Large Language Models", the author, Tomer Solberg, presents Sable, an argument system and single-GPU prover; the proof batches several kinds of checks into one sumcheck per domain and commits to every witness tensor and weight in one multilinear commitment, and the prover streams the model layer by layer on one GPU [1]. (General explanation added by this site: sumcheck is a family of interactive proof protocols that let a verifier check the result of a large sum with relatively little computation.)

Why it matters: on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, Sable proves all 64 layers of Qwen2.5-32B in 92.5 s for 4,096 tokens and 398 s for 16,384 tokens, and also covers Qwen3-32B and the mixture-of-experts Qwen3-30B-A3B (4,096 tokens in 121.8 and 84.7 s) [1]. A proof costs 41 to 50 times the serving time, or 6.4 cents per 4,096-token request at public GPU prices, and takes 40 to 64 times less time per parameter and token than zkLLM [1]. A verifier checks a proof on one CPU core in 19 to 56 s [1].

Where it sticks: the abstract states that the proof is not zero-knowledge, so the system targets open-weight models [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a zero-knowledge proof gives the verifier nothing beyond the fact that the statement holds; not being zero-knowledge means checking involves the model weights; open-weight models are models whose parameters are publicly released.) The integer function’s output is not identical to the BF16 model’s: on WikiText-2, its greedy token matches the BF16 model’s at 86.3% of positions; on Qwen3, it is within 1.6 points of BF16 on MMLU and 1.7 points on GSM8K [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other models or other hardware, can bring the proving time for bit-exact large language model inference proofs within about 50 times the serving time, or the proof cost to within about 10 cents per 4,096-token request.

**Item 2: When people say AI assistants are “getting dumber”, is it the models that change, or the people judging them?**

Background: users often complain that AI assistants such as ChatGPT are getting worse. The authors note that some versions do change, but human evaluation leaves a second possibility untested: the people judging may change [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-10, "AI Isn't Getting Dumber, We're Getting Pickier: Rising Standards in Human Evaluation of Large Language Models" (entry states working paper), three authors—Zihan Chen, Lei Nico Zheng and Di Zhu—separate the two on Chatbot Arena, a public platform where people compare answers from two anonymous large language models and pick the better one or say both are bad; they study 1,260,967 blind votes cast from May 2023 to August 2024 on pairs of models whose versions are fixed, namely dated vendor snapshots and models with fixed open weights [2].

Why it matters: for the same pair of unchanged models, the share of votes saying “both are bad” rises by 1.71 percentage points a month on a mean of 17.1%, and by 1.91 points a month in 2024; in fourteen pairs observed often in both 2023 and 2024, it rose in every one, from 15.5% to 23.3% [2]. Meanwhile the ranking does not trend: in 2024, the stronger model’s share of decisive votes is flat [2]. The abstract says the rise holds within the same voter and on the same pair, with prompt controls, in every major language, and between interface changes [2].

Where it sticks: the data come from one platform, Chatbot Arena, and end in August 2024 [2]. That standards rise with the progress of the whole category is the authors’ reading of the pattern: the abstract says the pattern fits such a standard, so a product that stands still is judged as declining [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other human-evaluation platforms or in later periods, also find for fixed-version model pairs that absolute verdicts (such as the share of “both are bad” votes) keep getting stricter while relative rankings stay largely unchanged.

**Item 3: When the same business procedure runs again and again, can a large language model agent be distilled into a workflow in which code picks the next step, trading for cost and speed?**

Background: business process automation runs the same Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) again and again on new inputs. Large language model agents can follow an SOP written in natural language, but they call a large language model to choose every step of every run, which adds latency and cost at scale [3].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-10, "ReTrace: Distilling Tool-Using Agents into Planner-Free Workflows", nine authors—Kaiyuan Hou, Yang Ping Kuo, Indranil Bhattacharya, Rohith Nama, Arushi Gupta, Carlos D. Castillo, Prathap Ramachandra, Xiaofan Jiang and Francesco Carbone—present ReTrace, which turns an SOP and a few logged agent runs into a reusable workflow in which code, not a large language model, selects the next step; it extracts the condition behind each step from the explanation the agent writes with each tool call, merges these conditions across runs into a compact rule document, and generates an executable workflow from it [3].

Why it matters: on 79 procedures from the authors’ production platform, ReTrace workflows reach 93.8% completion, against 96.8% for the agent, at 24% lower cost per task [3]. On τ²-Bench, they reach 75.8% task success (agent: 80.6%) and run in 1.3 s instead of 170 s [3].

{{< industry-link item="3" industry="ai" side="cost" inference="lower cost per task and shorter run time match an actual drop in inference spending for this kind of process-automation service" >}}The source reports the per-task cost cut of the workflows versus the agent and the run times of both [3], which maps to inference cost for running business procedures with large language models in this industry.{{< /industry-link >}}

Where it sticks: the workflows’ completion and success rates are both slightly below the agent’s own [3]. On eight SOP-Bench procedures, ReTrace without repair trails few-shot generation (80.6% vs. 83.6%); repair from labeled training failures lifts it to 87.8% at 0.40 s per task, compared to 85.5% in 46.6 s for the agent [3]. The 79 procedures come from the authors’ own production platform [3].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other sets of business procedures or other orchestration platforms, can turn agent run logs into workflows in which code picks the step and keep a per-task cost cut of about one-fifth while staying within about 3 percentage points of the agent’s completion rate.
