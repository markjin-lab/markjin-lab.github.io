Another paper shows that channel selection for mixture-of-experts (MoE) decoding needs no calibration data: a reader built from the model’s weights retains 98.1% of full-model accuracy at 17.8% of routed-expert reads across three post-trained MoE models, with Triton kernels running MoE blocks 1.60–1.90× faster than optimized vLLM at batch one [2].

**Item 1: When the agent interface changes harness, does a trained policy still carry the old preferences?**

Background: language-model agents are often post-trained and deployed through a harness of prompts, tools and control logic; harnesses are frequently revised and judged by how a fixed model performs under them, which assumes that what the model learned under one harness carries over to the next [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a harness here usually means an execution frame around a model that owns prompts, tool calls and step control; a policy usually means the rule by which the model chooses actions under a given interface.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-27, “Your Policy Is Secretly a Harness: The Decision Geometry of Agent Interfaces”, three authors—Jian Mu, Ruibo Shen and Yao Shu—show under KL-regularized control how the log odds of an optimal policy recover harness preferences over actions, write deployed return as harness ceiling minus policy mismatch, and test what harness changes and fixed-model comparisons mean on MiniWoB++ and Terminal-Bench Lite; code is noted in the abstract [1].

Why it matters: reversing the order of the observation fields leaves every optimal decision unchanged yet lowers a trained model’s success from 85.0% to 19.2% [1]. Conversely, every new harness that a fixed-model comparison rejects but that raises the ceiling outperforms the original agent after further training under it, and ceiling and mismatch measured beforehand predict these gains [1]. For coding-agent harnesses on Terminal-Bench Lite, ceiling minus mismatch estimated with a stronger model as a stand-in for the optimum reproduces the accuracy ranking, while either term alone does not [1].

Where it sticks: the abstract reports one MiniWoB++ success contrast under a field-order reversal and the predictive claim for ceiling and mismatch, without a full per-harness score table or a dollar or GPU-hour cost gap [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other agent environments or other backbone and harness setups, still find that a trained policy carries the preferences of its training harness so that interface perturbations that leave optimal decisions unchanged still collapse success, or that ceiling-raising harnesses rejected by fixed-model comparison still beat the original agent after further training under them.

**Item 2: For mixture-of-experts decoding, does channel selection still need offline calibration data?**

Background: mixture-of-experts (MoE) models are often bottlenecked by loading routed-expert weights from memory during interactive decoding at small batch sizes; because most intermediate channels in a routed expert contribute little to any single token, channel selectors typically keep channels above a threshold calibrated on offline data [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a mixture-of-experts model usually means an architecture that routes each input to a subset of expert subnetworks; channel selection usually means reading only the weight channels judged important inside those experts.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-27, “Channel Selection for Mixture-of-Experts Decoding Needs No Calibration Data”, two authors—Geunhyoung Kim and Sung Ju Hwang—argue that a calibrated threshold neither fixes the actual read budget nor yields a consistent accuracy gain at that budget, and introduce a reader built entirely from the model’s weights that screens channels with a ternary sketch and recovers skipped ones with a low-rank map; code is noted in the arXiv comment [2].

Why it matters: at the calibrated budget, a selector’s top-k version matches or beats its threshold version on the same score in 40 of 43 comparisons at 13% and 18% budgets, including all 23 on generation tasks [2]. Across three post-trained MoE models, the reader retains 98.1% of full-model accuracy at 17.8% of routed-expert reads, with Triton kernels running MoE blocks 1.60–1.90× faster than optimized vLLM at batch one and up to 2.69× at larger batches on the same three models [2].

**Item 3: For continuous diffusion language models, can the denoising trajectory itself supply the supervision?**

Background: continuous flow-based language models are a promising alternative to autoregressive generation, yet their training objectives typically rely on supervision from clean target tokens and leave the denoising trajectory underexploited as a learning signal [3]. (General explanation added by this site: a diffusion or flow language model usually means a model that generates text by iterative denoising or continuous interpolation rather than strictly left to right; self-distillation usually means retraining a student with a teacher signal from the same model family without an external pretrained teacher.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-27, “Self-Regularized Flow Language Models”, three authors—Theodoros Kouzelis, Spyros Gidaris and Nikos Komodakis—introduce SERF, which uses noisy-to-clean self-distillation along the same interpolation path with an exponential-moving-average teacher that receives a cleaner version of the student’s noisy input, and study representation-alignment and output-distribution-alignment variants; all variants augment the standard training objective without external pretrained models or changes to inference [3].

Why it matters: across three continuous language-model paradigms, SERF improves training efficiency, generation quality and few-step generation; it achieves up to 7.7× wall-clock training acceleration on OpenWebText, improves MAUVE by 24.3% and GSM8K accuracy by up to 28.8%, and matches baseline performance with up to 64× fewer sampling steps [3].
