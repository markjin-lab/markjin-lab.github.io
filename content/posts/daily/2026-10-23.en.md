**Item 1: Can monitorability be restored as a training target?**

Background: monitoring the chain of thought of language models is one of the few practical tools for overseeing capable AI systems, but it is fragile: the reasoning of a model is only useful to a monitor if it remains coupled to what the model actually does [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a chain of thought usually means the step-by-step reasoning text a model writes before giving its answer; monitorability usually means how well an observer can detect misbehavior by a model by reading its reasoning text.) This coupling can be degraded during training through, for example, CoT compression, optimization against a monitor, and out-of-context generalization from synthetic documents [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-21, “Monitorability Is Trainable: Post-Training for Chain-of-Thought Monitorability”, four authors, including Usman Anwar, propose an unsupervised post-training objective based on maximizing mutual information between the chain of thought and the output, for improving chain-of-thought monitorability [1]. (General explanation added by this site: mutual information usually means a measure of how much two variables share, or how much each reveals about the other; post-training usually means one or more further training stages after the main training of a model is done.)

Why it matters: across three model organisms with degraded chains of thought, the objective recovers most or all of the lost monitorability [1]. Applied to undegraded policies, it further improves judged monitorability, improves prediction of unsafe behavior by chain-of-thought monitors and activation probes, and increases the prevalence of consequential reasoning [1]. The authors therefore suggest that monitorability should be treated as a trainable attribute of the policy, and that a dedicated monitorability post-training stage can extend current practices that protect the chain of thought from direct optimization pressure [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on larger production models and under training setups beyond chain-of-thought compression or optimization against a monitor, can also restore or raise monitorability with a similar post-training objective.

**Item 2: Is the model reasoning, or recognizing textbook wording?**

Background: many NP-complete problems have a polynomial-time twin that differs in a single structural detail, such as 3- versus 2-colouring a graph [2]. (General explanation added by this site: NP-complete usually refers to a class of decision problems widely regarded as hard to solve exactly in polynomial time; polynomial time usually means an algorithm whose steps grow as a polynomial in the size of the input, and is therefore usually treated as efficient.) A model that reasons rather than recalls should notice which side of this boundary a problem falls on, yet existing benchmarks either fix the wording of each task or ignore its complexity class, so they cannot separate the two [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-21, “Did the Problem Actually Get Harder? Testing LLMs on Paired P and NP-Complete Problems Hardened by Adversarial Multi-Agent Rewriting”, five authors, including Nurul Labib Sayeedi, introduce P2NPCBench, a benchmark of 12 problem families in which every NP-complete problem is paired with a polynomial-time twin posed over the same surface [2]. To remove the textbook cues that enable recall, they build it with AgentGAN, an adversarial multi-agent framework in which a generator rewrites each statement until a panel of frozen code models fails, while an executable validity gate keeps every rewrite answer- and class-preserving [2]. The result is 2,114 hardened statements with hand-verified reference human solvers [2].

Why it matters: evaluating 12 proprietary and open-source models, every model scores lower on NP-complete statements than on their polynomial-time twins (35.4% vs. 63.8% pass@1), and 95.1% of model-family pairs fail more often on the NP-complete side [2]. A tenfold time limit raises NP-complete accuracy only from 28.5% to 31.9%, so failures stem from wrong or crashing programs rather than slow ones [2]. Hardening also narrows the class gap from 24.8 to 14.6 points, showing that part of the apparent gap reflects familiarity with textbook phrasing rather than complexity class [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, after removing textbook phrasing, still see a stable gap between how models do on the NP-complete side and on the polynomial-time side.

**Item 3: Once the rationale is written, does the answer still follow it?**

Background: chain-of-thought prompting improves the reasoning performance of large language models, but recent studies have shown that final answers do not always depend on the generated rationales; models may rely on query-specific shortcuts rather than the information in their reasoning steps [3]. (General explanation added by this site: a query shortcut usually means a model jumping to an answer from surface cues in the question, instead of relying on its reasoning steps.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-21, “Mitigating Query Shortcuts in Chain-of-Thought with Context-Aware Decoding”, one author, Shin'ya Yamaguchi, asks whether context-aware decoding, an existing contrastive decoding method, can mitigate such shortcut behavior when applied to chain-of-thought reasoning [3]. (General explanation added by this site: contrastive decoding usually means looking at next-token probabilities under two conditions at once and amplifying the tokens favored by the difference.) The author studies this application as Rationale Contrastive Decoding (RCD), which contrasts the next-token distributions conditioned on the query with and without its rationale, amplifying tokens whose likelihood increases due to the rationale [3].

Why it matters: experiments across five reasoning benchmarks show that RCD improves answer accuracy over standard chain-of-thought [3]. Intervention analyses using ground-truth and random rationales further show that RCD increases the behavioral dependence of final answers on the rationale content [3]. The author suggests that contrastive decoding offers a simple, training-free approach to mitigating query shortcuts [3].
