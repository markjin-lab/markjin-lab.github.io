A new benchmark has coding agents design specialized large language model serving systems and scores them by latency-qualified token value per dollar of GPU cost; Claude Sonnet 5.5 reaches 2.97 dollars per dollar on Gemma E4B, 4.87 times the vLLM reference [1]. In another benchmark of 56 end-to-end research tasks written by 42 physicists from their own research, none of 26 configurations across 22 models passes more than 46 of its 168 rollouts [2].

## Today’s news through the Unified Systems View

**Item 1: Can coding agents design a more cost-effective serving system for large language models, including the ones they run on?**

Background: The abstract says large language model coding agents develop software through experimentation, which gives them an opportunity to design the serving systems that support their own operation; effective serving requires coordinating implementation and operating decisions under session dependencies, latency constraints and correctness requirements [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-04, "ServerBench: How can your coding agents design specialized LLM serving systems?", eight authors—Xinrui Zhong, Zhiming Wang, Yongqi Liu, Zhaohui Wang, Shanglin Yang, Zikun Li, Zhuoming Chen and Beidi Chen—introduce ServerBench, a benchmark for agentic design of complete, specialized large language model serving systems; agents experiment against a common objective, dollars per dollar, meaning latency-qualified token value per dollar of GPU cost, while stateful replay preserves session dependencies and independent verification checks the submitted system [1].

Why it matters: Across 11 coding-agent models and three deployments, Claude Sonnet 5.5 achieves 2.97 dollars per dollar on Gemma E4B, 4.87 times the vLLM reference; 22 of 33 runs yield verified systems, and 13 exceed the strongest baseline [1].

{{< industry-link item="1" industry="ai" side="cost" inference="cost efficiency on this benchmark matches actual GPU spending in inference services" >}}The source reports latency-qualified token value per dollar of GPU cost and the multiple over the vLLM reference [1], which maps to GPU cost for large language model inference services in this industry.{{< /industry-link >}}

Where it sticks: 11 of the 33 runs did not yield a verified system, and the authors’ analysis notes that reliable delivery requires verifying the execution paths introduced by optimization [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other models or deployments, can have coding agents design serving systems that reach several times the token value per dollar of a common reference implementation while most runs pass independent verification.

**Item 2: How far are AI agents from carrying out a real physics research project end to end?**

Background: The abstract says AI agents play an increasingly important role in physics research, yet existing physics benchmarks only evaluate fixed questions, isolated sub-tasks or paper reproduction with given solutions, and rarely check research quality alongside correctness [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-04, "FrontierPhysics: Evaluating Agents for End-to-End Physics Research", 72 authors led by Bingran You, Wenjun Ke and Ria Rosenauer introduce FrontierPhysics, an end-to-end benchmark of 56 tasks made by 42 physicists from their own research, covering nine domains across theoretical, computational and experimental physics; each task requires agents to review the literature, develop a research plan, conduct research and experiments, and produce structured outputs and scientific manuscripts, and evaluation combines deterministic verifiers with expert-authored rubrics to judge whether the research is correct and publishable [2].

Why it matters: Across 22 models in 26 configurations, no configuration passes more than 46 of its 168 rollouts [2]. Manual review of agent trajectories shows that frontier agents’ main weaknesses are insufficiently rigorous reasoning and limited expert intuition, rather than gaps in physics knowledge or execution errors [2].

Where it sticks: The abstract gives an upper bound on passes and qualitative findings from manual review, without full per-model scores [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, building end-to-end research benchmarks in other fields or with more tasks, also find frontier agents passing under about three in ten attempts, with weaknesses again centred on rigor of reasoning rather than domain knowledge.
