Three research leads examine how model serving can free GPU memory, how fibre calibration can use a few real measurements, and how simulated home energy savings hold up in practice. The four preprints were first submitted to arXiv on November 19, 2026 (UTC). This issue draws on the metadata and authors’ abstracts [1], [2], [3], [4].

## Freeing GPU memory when serving hybrid models

TideCache, by Jinbin Luo and colleagues, targets language models that use both global attention and bounded-state layers. While a bounded-state layer runs, the system fetches the next global-attention layer’s prefix cache from host memory into shared GPU slots. Newly generated key-value cache entries stay on the GPU. Transfers can overlap with computation, and the prefix cache can occupy less GPU memory [1].

The authors tested seven model–platform configurations on GB300 and GH200. Compared with SGLang using HiCache, they report a 20%–59% reduction in overall median per-token latency. The result applies to the models, hardware and workloads tested. The abstract does not give the workload mix, transfer overhead or latency distribution for each configuration. To assess an existing service, a next check would compare GPU memory use and median and high-percentile latency on the same hardware and workloads while varying context length and concurrency [1].

## How the amount of measured data changes the choice of fibre calibration method

Callum J. Wilson and colleagues compared an autoencoder pretrained on simulations with principal component analysis (PCA) for multimode fibre transmission matrices. For a 50-micrometre fibre at 62-fold compression, adapting the autoencoder with two measured matrices produced 98% of the ideal focusing enhancement. That metric measures focusing performance [2].

With PCA fitted on all 163 measured matrices, the authors report matrix errors on held-out matrices of 1.1% for PCA and 11.9% for the autoencoder. The authors also report that PCA was already more accurate with 10 measured matrices, and that the autoencoder transferred only partially to a different fibre. Focusing enhancement and matrix error need separate comparisons. The authors have yet to test whether they can recover a matrix with fewer measurements than a full matrix requires. That is the next step in finding out whether the method can reduce calibration work [2].

## What home energy control still has to prove in practice

Julian Ruddick and colleagues simulated 500 representative electrified Belgian dwellings. They assessed 2023 energy costs under a real electricity contract with hourly prices and a monthly peak tariff. Compared with rule-based control that mimicked default operation, the simulation gave average annual savings per dwelling of €82.5 for the TreeC decision-tree controller and €116.6 for model predictive control. The authors regard those savings as modest relative to the purchase and subscription costs [3].

In a separate abstract, the same first author reports that both methods performed worse on real hardware than in simulations. The author attributes the gap for TreeC to insufficiently representative training data and the gap for model predictive control to unreliable real-time inputs. The two abstracts provide no field savings figures that can be directly compared. A next check would use actual bills under the same tariff and the full cost of control equipment to assess net savings alongside comfort and charging completion [3], [4].
