**Item 1: After writing latency, throughput and memory into the pruning objective, can accuracy still rise relative to a parameter-count-only pipeline?**

Background: LLM pruning must preserve quality while meeting hardware- and workload-specific constraints on latency, throughput and memory. Existing workflows often target parameter counts and leave practitioners to pick sizes that fit latency, throughput and GPU-memory limits by hand; parameter count alone does not predict those metrics, which also depend on architecture, workload, hardware and the serving engine [1]. (General explanation added by this site: pruning usually means removing or zeroing some parameters to shrink a model, and differentiable architecture search uses continuous relaxations to search structure during training.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "AutoCompress: Automated Hardware-Guided Model Compression", seven authors—Sheldon Liang, Ali Taghibakhshi, Saurav Muralidharan, Sharath Turuvekere Sreenivas, Ashwath Aithal, Pavlo Molchanov and Nima Tajbakhsh—introduce AutoCompress, a hardware-guided compression framework that writes those constraints into differentiable architecture search, jointly optimizes global and per-layer structure into heterogeneous architectures, and is guided by serving-engine cost models validated against end-to-end latency, throughput and memory measurements [1].

Why it matters: compared with the state-of-the-art Minitron pruning framework, AutoCompress raises average accuracy across eight benchmarks by 6.57, 2.35 and 0.57 percentage points on RTX 5070, RTX 5080 and H100, respectively [1]. It also compresses Nemotron 3.5 Super from 120B to 74B parameters for single-B200 BF16 serving at 128K context, retaining 95% of its mean benchmark score [1].

**Item 2: After low-bit quantization loses accuracy, can extra precision from host RAM and solid-state drives pull quality back without stalling generation?**

Background: quantization lowers the memory and bandwidth demands of LLM inference but can degrade accuracy; offloading higher-precision data can instead stall generation. The authors introduce StrataFlux to recover accuracy lost to low-bit weight and key-value (KV) cache quantization while retaining quantization’s memory and speed advantages [2]. (General explanation added by this site: quantization usually means storing weights or activations with fewer bits, and a key-value cache holds intermediate states during generation for later steps to reuse.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "StrataFlux: Memory-Hierarchy-Native Low-Bit LLM Inference", four authors—Jake Hyun, Ziyu Zhao, Jae-sun Seo and Mohamed S. Abdelfattah—co-design refinement representation, selection and delivery so host RAM and solid-state drives become useful sources of additional precision; selective precision streaming cuts transfer volume, overlaps delivery with computation, and reuses fetched precision across decoding steps so generation need not wait for every transfer [2].

Why it matters: for 2-bit Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct weights, StrataFlux reduces WikiText-2 perplexity from 9.46 to 8.40 while maintaining 95.4% of the quantized base’s throughput on RTX 5090 [2]. With 2-bit weights and KV, it raises Qwen3-8B’s AIME 2024/2025 accuracy from 11.0% to 47.1% at unchanged GPU memory use [2]. At 128K context, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with 3-bit weights and 2-bit KV achieves comparable perplexity and speed to vLLM’s 4-bit AWQ weights and 8-bit KV on RTX 5090, using approximately half the GPU memory [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other model families or other host/SSD tiers, still find that selective precision streaming from the offload tier can pull perplexity or task accuracy back while keeping most of the quantized-base throughput, or cut GPU memory sharply at comparable quality and speed.

**Item 3: If test-time reasoning resamples only near a likely first error, can it match self-consistency accuracy on average while using fewer tokens?**

Background: self-consistency (SC) is widely used to improve chain-of-thought reasoning, but it samples many complete trajectories under a preset budget. Efficient variants mainly stop full-trajectory sampling early or spread samples across questions. The authors propose StepBack: given a small set of draft trajectories, it localizes likely first-error positions with lightweight signals, resamples that step and its suffix, then majority-votes the answer [3]. (General explanation added by this site: self-consistency usually means drawing repeated reasonings for a question and voting on the answer, and a token is a text unit for a model.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "StepBack: Step-Level Error-Localized Resampling for Efficient Test-Time Reasoning", two authors—Yang Ouyang and Jung-Eun Kim—present the method; the arXiv entry states NeurIPS 2026 [3].

Why it matters: across seven mathematical-reasoning and multi-hop QA benchmarks, StepBack matches SC accuracy on average while reducing token usage by up to 41.26% [3]. It can also be combined with adaptive inference methods such as Adaptive Consistency, further improving inference-time efficiency [3].
