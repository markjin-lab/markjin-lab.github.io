**Item 1: FDE-Gym, a benchmark that tests AI agents on delivering projects for customers.**

Background: most existing tests of agents (AI programs that carry out tasks from text instructions) hand them well-specified tasks, such as issue reports, specifications or task tickets [1]. Real customer delivery is different: forward-deployed engineers (engineers sent to a customer to put an AI system into real use) must clarify ambiguous requirements, work in live systems and uncover constraints the customer never stated [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “FDE-Gym: The Last Mile of AI Delivery”, 26 authors, including Dongxu Yang, release a benchmark of 95 end-to-end delivery tasks drawn from 20 customer scenarios across 18 sectors, each adapted by industry experts from real delivery projects [1]. In simulated customer environments, agents talk with customer-side stakeholders who follow a script and work on production-like systems to deliver a working solution [1]. A delivery is scored by how much it improves the customer's own success metric, with 0 for an unchanged system and 1 for the reference solution, and with penalties for messages to stakeholders, trial runs and waiting time [1].

Why it matters: the authors test 13 model configurations from seven providers [1]. In the realistic setting, where unstated constraints must be uncovered, the best configuration has a mean score of 0.56 and resolves only 14 of the 95 tasks, where resolving means a score of at least 0.8 with no incident [1]. The authors identify uncovering unstated constraints as the main bottleneck: without explicit guidance, performance drops sharply [1]. That is not the whole story, though: on the 23 tasks where the best configuration obtained every key fact from the stakeholders, it still reached a normalized improvement of 0.8 or more in the customer's metric on only 10 [1].

What changed: earlier agent tests mostly measured getting a well-specified task right; this study also tests working out what the customer actually needs and then delivering on a production-like system, and finds a wide gap between the two [1].

Where it is stuck: the tasks and customer environments are simulated, the stakeholders follow a script, and the reference solution defines full marks; whether the gap is as wide in real customer projects still needs new evidence [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether later tests of the same kind show agents narrowing the gap above on tasks where requirements are unclear and constraints must be drawn out.

**Item 2: CATCH, a training method aimed at language model honesty.**

Background: the authors note that language models are increasingly used where honesty is critical, yet after alignment training (training that makes a model behave as people intend) they still make claims that contradict what they know or that the context does not support [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “CAT-alyzing Honesty: Continuous Adversarial Training for Transferable LLM Honesty”, four authors, including Yuan-Chen Chang, propose a training method called CATCH [2]. The abstract does not explain the mechanics of 'continuous adversarial training' in the title; it says CATCH trains the model toward an honest answer and away from a dishonest one while maintaining its capabilities [2]. It borrows from a method first built to make models reliably refuse harmful requests, training the model toward an honest answer and away from a dishonest one while keeping its existing abilities; the authors evaluate it on two models, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen3-3.6-27B [2].

Why it matters: with only a few hundred training targets built from self-reporting scenarios, the honest behavior CATCH learns carries over to several distinct tests [2]. The paper uses MASK to test whether a model gives dishonest answers under pressure, and AuditBench to test whether a model's hidden behaviors can be found [2]. It reduces dishonest answers under pressure on the MASK test, increases confessions in the multi-agent game Among Us, and raises the rate at which a model's hidden behaviors are found in AuditBench [2]. The abstract reports that CATCH increased the model's confessions in the multi-agent game Among Us, but does not explain how the game tests or counts confessions [2]. The authors report that CATCH improves honesty more than prompting and supervised fine-tuning, matches the best current honesty activation steering (a method that directly adjusts a model's internal activations), and does better at keeping the model useful [2].

**Item 3: Language model text scoring and rating scale design.**

Background: in computational social science (the study of social phenomena with computational methods), researchers increasingly use language models to score social and psychological concepts in text; but studies use different rating scales, and whether scores stay comparable across scales has received little study [3].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “Change the Number, Change the Answer: Assessing Rating Scale Sensitivity in LLM-Based Text Scoring”, three authors, including Wonjeong Jo, systematically examine how rating scale design changes the scores [3]. The experiments cross 2 formats (continuous or ordinal), 4 ranges (3, 5, 11 and 21 points) and 2 signs (signed or unsigned), across 3 tasks (political leaning, sentiment and toxicity, meaning offensive or harmful language), 2 prompting strategies and 5 language models [3].

Why it matters: Changing one element of the scale design shifts what the paper calls 'normalized scores' by about 0.10 on average and changes the category of roughly 20% of texts; the abstract does not explain the conversion [3]. Toxicity scoring is the most sensitive, and toxicity is the only unipolar concept in the study (it runs from none to a lot, with no opposite pole) [3]. The paper compares zero-shot prompting with prompting that supplies a scoring rubric; the rubric does not reduce this sensitivity, and the abstract does not describe how the zero-shot prompts were written [3].
