The main story today is a study of ten large-language-model agents (AI programs that carry out tasks from text instructions) discussing factual questions: one adversary repeatedly asserting a false answer raised the share of ordinary agents giving that answer privately at the end from 0.6% to 44.8% [1]. Another paper models one upstream language-model provider and several competing downstream firms, finding that downstream safety investment is generally above the level that maximizes industry profit, while the upstream provider may invest below the paper's reference levels [2]. Two further papers report separate results: five tested commercial language models use the Chinese “not …, but …” construction 2.5 to 9.2 times as densely as human reference text [3]; a specially trained small model scores higher than Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-6 Astra on combined idea quality and diversity in the authors' own test [4].

**Item 1: When AIs check one another, one member “pushing a line” can lead the group astray.** Background: more and more AI systems have a group of agents discuss, critique and check one another, assuming that peers will catch an individual member's errors [1]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “Believed but Not Said: Measuring Hidden Belief Collapse in Multi-Agent LLM Discussions”, six authors, including Liangjie Huang, test this assumption with the simplest kind of adversary: ten agents discuss factual questions while one or three “adversaries” repeatedly assert a plausible false answer [1]. After each round, the researchers ask each ordinary member in a separate call that the others cannot see, to find its private answer, and separately label the stance it takes publicly in the discussion [1]. Why it matters: across six models and three factual question-answering datasets, only 0.6% of ordinary members end up with the false answer when there is no adversary; one adversary raises this to 44.8%, and three raise it to 60.5% [1]. With three adversaries present, ordinary members privately hold the false answer in 51.6% of exposed turns but publicly endorse it in only 19.7% [1]. A turn is one ordinary member's message in a round, and it counts as exposed if at least one adversary's message comes before it in the discussion transcript [1]. Members that keep the true answer often fail to defend it; once adopted, the false answer spreads among ordinary members, and how much a member knew to begin with does not predict which ones resist being led astray [1]. What changed: having several agents check one another is often treated as a way to catch errors; this study shows that reading the discussion alone underestimates how far members are led astray, because much of the influence happens in private and cannot be seen in the discussion [1]. Where it is stuck: the study covers only factual question answering in ten-member discussions, and the adversaries only repeat the same false answer; whether the same holds in multi-agent systems inside real products still needs new evidence [1]. Which trends it connects to: more and more systems rely on several agents discussing, reviewing and checking one another to reduce errors, which is exactly the premise this study tests [1]. What to watch next: by the end of 2026, whether another research team measures agents' private answers separately from their public stances on different models or tasks and finds a similar result, with private changes of answer outnumbering public agreement [1].

**Item 2: Does competition make AI companies spend more on safety?** Background: the author notes that modern AI systems pose societal risks such as harmful content, malicious use and misinformation, and that reducing these risks depends not only on whether it is technically feasible but also on whether the firms that develop and deploy these systems have reason to do it [2]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “Market Incentives for AI Safety Investment”, Nikita Tsoy uses a game-theoretic model (a mathematical model of how parties decide when their choices affect one another) to study an AI supply chain with one upstream model provider and several competing downstream firms [2]. The author derives the level of safety investment the market produces on its own and compares it with the levels that maximize market welfare and industry profit [2]. Why it matters: the paper finds that downstream competition generally leads downstream firms to invest more in safety than the level that maximizes industry profit [2]. By contrast, the upstream provider may invest less than these reference levels, because it does not receive all of the benefit its safety investment creates [2]. The author also analyzes how different regulatory interventions change each party's incentives, its profits and the resulting level of model safety [2].

**Item 3: Language models writing Chinese love “not …, but …”; mostly it is not wrong, just overused.** Background: expressions that recur in model-written text are often treated as signs that a machine wrote it, but the authors argue that most of them serve legitimate purposes; whether an expression counts as a verbal tic depends on whether the model knows when it is called for [3]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “Not X, but When: Diagnosing a Mandarin LLM Writing Tic through Contextual Appropriateness”, five authors, including Yihang Peng, take the corrective construction “not X, but Y” as their subject and design a step-by-step set of rules: they check whether the context makes X an option a reader might really have in mind and whether rejecting X adds information, and on that basis sort each use into necessary, optional or wrong [3]. Why it matters: five commercial models use this construction 2.5 to 9.2 times as densely as human reference text [3]. Of 307 hand-annotated uses, only 1.30% are wrong, while 52.8% are optional, that is, correct but unneeded; so models fall short on whether a use is appropriate far more often than on whether it is correct [3]. The authors also find that several reward models (models that score answers during training) favor the construction even over rewrites of matched quality, that a language model acting as a judge favors it even where the context does not call for it, and that two common training methods tend to make open-weight models (models whose weights are published and can be downloaded and run) use it more often [3].

**Item 4: Training a small model so that its research ideas do not repeat one another.** Background: the first step of research is often coming up with ideas; more and more work uses large language models to automate this step, but most of it pursues the quality of individual ideas and overlooks whether the ideas differ from one another [4]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-15, “Training Reasoning Models to Generate Diverse Scientific Ideas”, six authors, including Manya Wadhwa, have a model read existing papers and write, in one go, several idea cards grounded in those papers [4]. In training, the whole set of ideas is scored together, with each idea's quality score weighted by how different it is from the ideas written before it, which teaches the model to steer each new idea away from earlier ones [4]. Why it matters: on the authors' own test set built from 2026 target papers, their trained 8-billion-parameter model (parameter count measures model size) scores higher than Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-6 Astra on “creative utility”, which combines quality and diversity [4]. In a pilot human evaluation with four experts, this model's sets of ideas were judged as promising as Claude Opus 4.8's and contained slightly more unique ideas [4]. When a coding agent ran small proof-of-concept experiments on the ideas, the success rate was comparable to Claude Opus 4.8's, from which the authors conclude that the added diversity did not cost executability [4].

**X1. NIST (@NIST) posts about the CGPM resolution on continuous UTC**

Background: NIST is the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the CGPM is the General Conference on Weights and Measures. A leap second is a second inserted from time to time so that the world's common standard time stays in step with the Earth's rotation. The link in the post points to Resolution 5 of the 28th CGPM (2026), “On the technical actions needed to ensure the continuity of UTC”, on the BIPM website; it decides that “continuous UTC will become effective on 20 May 2027” and that the maximum difference between UTC and Earth-rotation time (UT1) will be 3 600 seconds (1 hour) [6]. The page notes that the official version is the French text, which says the same [7]. The quotations below are the post's own wording; “voted” and “officially eliminate leap seconds” are the post's summary and do not appear in the resolution [5].

> We have had our last leap second!

> The General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) voted to officially eliminate leap seconds, establishing continuous Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) effective May 20, 2027.
