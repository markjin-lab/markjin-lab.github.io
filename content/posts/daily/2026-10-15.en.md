The thing most worth knowing today: a paper says that when a “student” model is trained on a “teacher” model's answers, the student picks up the teacher's attitude toward harmful requests even when the questions have nothing to do with safety [1]. Three other papers examine AI agents (systems that choose actions and use tools): why a model checking dates and other tool inputs can be misled by a visible reference [2], how to test which stored lessons help [3], and how to estimate whether an attempt will succeed [4].

**Item 1: Distillation passes on more than skill.** Background: knowledge distillation trains one model (the “student”) on answers generated by another (the “teacher”), usually to transfer the ability to solve tasks [1]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-13, Yu-Chien Tang and An-Zi Yen turned the same base teacher into a “harm-accepting” version and a “harm-avoiding” version, had both answer ordinary reasoning and knowledge questions unrelated to safety, and used those answers to train students built on different base models [1]. Why it matters: the students generally learned the tasks, but their later decisions also shifted toward the teacher's balance between safety and pragmatism, which the authors call “imprinting” [1]. Distilling from the harm-accepting teacher reduced the students' harm avoidance; distilling from the harm-avoiding teacher increased it [1]. What changed: judging whether distillation data is “clean” cannot rest only on whether the questions touch safety topics; it also matters which teacher wrote the answers [1]. Where it is stuck: the strength of imprinting varies across teacher–student pairs and does not extend uniformly across different kinds of safety behavior [1]. Removing a small set of text pieces the authors call “divergence tokens” reduces imprinting, while rewriting the answers keeps the source-dependent difference and can add shifts that come from the rewriting model itself [1]. A further round of preference-based training after distillation makes students avoid harm more, but does not remove the difference caused by the source [1]. The link to item 2 is that item 1 studies how the source of training answers affects student behavior, while item 2 studies how a reference visible during judging affects a decision; both call for checking what the model actually relied on [1], [2]. What to watch next: watch whether, by the end of 2026, a major model provider or public dataset that releases a distilled model or distillation data names the teacher model and separately reports how the student's safety behavior changed [1].

**Item 2: Judges that check tool arguments follow whatever reference they can see.** Background: when an agent calls a tool it must fill in arguments, such as a date or a quantity; a tool-call judge is a separate model that decides whether those values are right [2]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-13, five authors, including Linhai Ma, report that when the judge's input contains no text saying what a value should be, the judge accepts correct values less often; they call this drop the “derivation gap” [2]. This happens in three situations: the value is a default from the function description that the user never mentioned, the value has to be inferred, or the text it comes from was cut from the judge's input [2]. Why it matters: all seven judge models they evaluated showed the gap [2]. In a controlled test that moved the source out of view, judges favored a visible incorrect value over a correct value whose source was hidden [2]. What changed: the authors propose DeRef (Derived Reference), in which a separate model reads the full conversation history without seeing the candidate answer and writes a reference for the judge [2]. Across three benchmarks (public test sets), DeRef improved every tested judge's ability to tell right arguments from wrong ones (measured by AUROC, where random guessing scores about 0.5 and perfect separation scores 1) [2]. On the controlled test, that measure rose from chance or below to between 98.40 and 99.59 (the paper reports it on a 100-point scale) [2]. Where it is stuck: all seven judges showed the derivation gap, but DeRef was tested with four in the main experiments and two more in supplementary experiments; the paper reports reference-generation usage and timing, while deployment cost still depends on the setting [2]. Which trends it connects to: item 1 likewise warns that models follow the conspicuous cues in their input [1], [2]. What to watch next: watch whether, by the end of 2026, a public agent-evaluation framework or judging tool adds an independently generated reference value to the judge's input, or separately reports acceptance rates for default and inferred values [2].

**Item 3: An agent's self-written lessons should be picked by randomized experiment.** Background: some agents write down a few lessons after each task and keep them as a “playbook” that they read into their prompt (the input text given to the model) next time, without retraining [3]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-13, five authors, including Hoang Pham, point out that current systems let the agent decide which lessons to keep, which makes results unstable: the same procedure can write a playbook that helps or one that scores up to 69.6 points below using no playbook [3]. They propose Randomized Block Curation (RBC): generate several playbooks, randomly test groups of lessons within each on practice tasks and keep those that help, then use a fresh round of practice runs to choose among the curated playbooks, the original whole playbooks, and no playbook [3]. Across two memory systems, three models and three benchmarks, RBC raised average success to 66.9, versus 55.5 with the whole playbook, 54.1 with no playbook and 53.8 when the agent itself picked the lessons [3]. Too little information for now; no framework reading yet [3].

**Item 4: Teaching an agent to foresee whether this attempt will fail.** Background: when an agent works on a long task, each step requires deciding whether to trust its next action, have it stop, or hand off to a person, which needs a step-by-step confidence that predicts whether this attempt will finally succeed [4]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-13, Ashutosh Hathidara proposes calibrating the confidence an agent states against the final outcome of the whole task, rather than against whether the current step is right [4]. In three text-based task environments, ALFWorld, TextWorld and WebShop, offline training on recorded runs of a Qwen2.5-7B agent raised the AUROC of step confidence for telling final success from failure from near chance to 0.68–0.85; the 10% of steps the agent was most confident about succeeded about 4.3 times as often as average [4]. In online training on a live ALFWorld agent, expected calibration error (the gap between stated confidence and actual success rate) fell from 0.42 to below 0.12, with no drop in task success [4]. The author also notes that the trained agent recognizes from its first step which tasks it will fail, while telling a succeeding attempt from a failing one at the same task remains harder [4]. Too little information for now; no framework reading yet [4].

> Students generally acquire task capability while their downstream decisions shift toward the source teacher's safety-pragmatism disposition, a phenomenon we call imprinting.

> These results show that behavioral tendencies can be intentionally imprinted through the source teacher, even with ordinary task supervision.
