Three research leads examine how to shorten hyperparameter searches, how to test cooperation between Transformer layers, and whether hive temperature can warn of colony collapse. The three preprints were first submitted to arXiv on November 20, 2026 (UTC). This issue draws on the metadata and authors’ abstracts [1], [2], [3].

## Count subset selection time when assessing a faster hyperparameter search

Mamoona Tasadduq and colleagues select a small subset of the full training data. The aim is to keep the relative ranking of hyperparameter configurations close to their ranking on the full data while limiting the size of the subset. On seven tabular, two text and two image benchmark datasets, the authors used three evolutionary algorithms to search for hyperparameters and report test accuracy comparable to searches on the full data. Speedups for the search stage ranged from about 1.14× to 8.80× across datasets and optimizers. The authors account for the one-time subset selection cost separately in their end-to-end runtime and break-even analysis [1].

On 20 configurations held out from subset construction, the method achieved higher ranking correlation than random sampling at the same subset size on eight of 11 datasets. The abstract does not give the selection cost or the number of repeated searches needed to break even for each task. A practical comparison would record total runtime for the first and subsequent searches and measure final accuracy on the same test set [1].

## Test how cooperation between layers develops at a given depth

JaeSeong Kim and colleagues use ablations to isolate the component of the model’s computation that appears only when a specified set of layers acts together. They track public training checkpoints from Pythia, OLMo and Amber. Across the interaction orders they measured, coordination strengthens as pretraining progresses relative to initialization or the earliest checkpoint. In all 35 model and interaction-order combinations measured, removing the jointly produced component worsened perplexity more than removing a norm-matched random direction at the same site [2].

In seven models, the authors also found a negative correlation between representation similarity and coordination for pairs of layers. When the two measures were used to choose blocks to remove, they often agreed on Pythia but diverged on Amber. A next check would remove the same number of layers and compare the resulting performance on the same tasks. The abstract gives no results from that comparison on specific downstream tasks [2].

## Hive temperature can show a trend while offering limited warning

Daniele Proverbio and colleagues combine dynamical models with longitudinal hive temperature observations to test whether indicators based on critical slowing down can warn of honey bee colony collapse. The models studied show regime shifts accompanied by critical slowing down. Detecting those signals in finite, noisy observations requires longer and more densely sampled time series than are typically available in practical monitoring. Similar trends were detectable in observed temperatures, but they provided limited separation between colonies that later collapsed and those that survived [3].

The authors report that falling mean temperature discriminated slightly better than conventional critical slowing down indicators, though its predictive value was still modest. Two quantities matter for practical monitoring under the same sampling conditions: how much warning time is available and what false-alarm rate is tolerable. The abstract provides no directly comparable values for either [3].
