An audit finds issues in roughly 30% of Terminal-Bench 2.0, a coding-agent benchmark that underwent extensive human and LM-assisted review before release; the repaired Terminal-Bench 2.1 raises accuracy by over 10 percentage points and moves leaderboard positions by up to three ranks [1]. Another study uses real data from the Belgian grid and an offshore wind farm to obtain an interpretable wind farm power control policy that cuts imbalance costs by 82% [2].

## Today’s news through the Unified Systems View

**Item 1: However carefully a benchmark is validated at release, can it go stale as models improve?**

Background: The abstract says coding agents are among the most economically consequential deployments of large language models, making reliable evaluation increasingly important, yet even benchmarks carefully validated at release can become stale: as models improve they can discover valid solutions that existing verifiers reject, and changes to evaluation infrastructure can invalidate assumptions embedded in tasks and tests [1]. The authors call the resulting accumulation of defects benchmark maintenance debt, a growing gap between benchmark scores and the capabilities they are intended to measure [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "Benchmarks as Software: Terminal-Bench and the Case for Continuous Benchmarks", four authors—E. Kelly Buchanan, Crystal Zhou, Scott W. Linderman and Christopher Ré—introduce a continuous-validation workflow for agentic benchmarks that combines community reports, task artifacts, agent traces, automated oracle checks and human-gated repairs, and apply it to Terminal-Bench 2.0 [1].

Why it matters: The audit identifies issues in roughly 30% of the benchmark, and the repairs are released as Terminal-Bench 2.1; they change evaluation results, raising accuracy by over 10 percentage points and moving leaderboard positions by up to three ranks, while the overall ranking remains highly correlated (Spearman rho = 0.958) [1]. The authors also find that a fixed wall-clock limit can materially change agent performance: for example, extending the time limit closes a 20-point harness gap on GPT 5.4 and shrinks a 30-point gap on GPT 5.4 mini to 5 points [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a wall-clock limit is a cap on run time measured in real elapsed time.)

Where it sticks: The abstract reports an audit of this one benchmark; that resource budgets are part of the measurement specification and must be validated alongside the tasks is the authors’ conclusion from it [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, auditing other agent benchmarks in a similar way, also find defects in roughly one-fifth to one-third of tasks, with scores shifting by around 10 percentage points after repair.

**Item 2: Can a wind farm control strategy learned by reinforcement learning be turned into transparent rules that people can read and justify?**

Background: The abstract says wind farm power control can help the system maintain balance and avoid blackouts; in Europe this can be done through implicit balancing, where a wind farm adjusts its power setpoint in anticipation of the imbalance price using information published by the Transmission System Operator, helping restore system balance while also reducing its own imbalance costs from forecast errors [2]. The difficulty is that the settlement price is unknown in advance and can be affected by the control strategy; reinforcement learning methods could help, but lack the interpretability needed to be deployed on real wind farms [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-05, "Value-oriented policy distillation for interpretable wind farm power control", five authors—Max Bruninx, Timothy Verstraeten, Jan Decuyper, Ann Nowé and Jan Helsen—propose a policy distillation framework using explainable boosting machines (EBMs), a variant of generalized additive models, to obtain a transparent control policy; inspired by energy forecasting research, they introduce a value-oriented perspective to policy distillation and include expert knowledge in model design so that the strategy is also justifiable to stakeholders [2]. (General explanation added by this site: policy distillation means training a simpler model to imitate an already trained, more complex policy.)

Why it matters: Using real data of the Belgian grid and an offshore wind farm, the authors find that their value-oriented, expert-based controller can reduce imbalance costs by 82%, while outperforming a price threshold controller and a policy distillation baseline using decision trees [2].

Where it sticks: The result comes from data for one grid and one offshore wind farm, and the abstract does not say whether the controller has been deployed on a real wind farm [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on data from other grids or wind farms, can use interpretable distilled policies for implicit balancing to cut imbalance costs by half or more while beating a simple price threshold rule.
