A study finds that language models labeling online comments for toxicity mistake the second-person “you” for toxicity, and that this bias manufactures patterns in the measurements that human labels do not show [1]. Another paper audits 33,000 trajectories from eleven small open-weight agents and finds that most prompt-injection attacks that failed were ignored by the agents rather than refused [2].

## Today's news through the Unified Systems View

For what the Unified Systems View is, see the [About page](/en/about/). All three papers in this issue can be read on arXiv, and none of their arXiv entries states publication or review status [1], [2], [3].

**Item 1: Using language models to label the toxicity of online comments.**

Background: much of what the Web research community believes about toxic conversations, such as that toxicity rises in longer and deeper threads, was measured mostly with Google's Perspective API, which is shutting down; the authors expect language models to make the next measurements [1]. (General explanation added by this site: toxicity in text usually refers to offensive, abusive or harmful language; Perspective API usually refers to an online service from Google that automatically gives text a toxicity score.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-16, “"You" Is Not Toxic: How LLM Labelers Rewrite the Web's Conversation Laws”, two authors, Liang Yue and Eric A. Vance, find that language model labelers mistake second-person address for toxicity [1]. On 1.25 million news comments with crowd labels, Claude Haiku flags 12% of the non-toxic comments that contain no “you” and 51% of those with four or more; a single crowd rater flags only 6% and 10% of these two groups [1].

Why it matters: deeper replies address someone more often, so under Haiku's labels toxicity rises with reply depth (0.93 percentage points per level), while under the crowd's labels it slightly declines (−0.12) [1]. Rewriting “you” in the third person removes two thirds of this false rise; having Haiku flag only as many comments as the crowd does not remove it, and showing Haiku the parent comment enlarges it [1]. All seven language models the authors test over-flag “you”, and Haiku does the same on Reddit, Gab and Wikipedia talk pages [1].

What changed: the authors point out that agreement between model labels and human labels does not reveal this bias [1]. They therefore propose a method called Threadsafe, which uses the labeler's scores to choose which comments human raters label and to predict the others, and then corrects the predictions with the human labels [1]. The authors report that a thousand human labels are enough to rule out Haiku's pattern of toxicity rising with reply depth [1].

Where it is stuck: the authors state that what Threadsafe estimates without bias is linear patterns, and their results on how many human labels it needs come from simulated audits [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams using language models to label online speech also report over-flagging of second-person address.

**Item 2: Small tool-using agents and indirect prompt injection.**

Background: agents that use tools read untrusted external content, which exposes them to indirect prompt injection, where hidden instructions hijack the agent's tool calls [2]. Benchmarks measure this kind of security by the attack success rate, which treats every failed attack as a successful defense [2].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-16, “Ignored, Not Refused: What Low Prompt-Injection Success Rates Hide in Small Tool-Using Agents”, seven authors, including Farhan Zarif, audit 33,000 trajectories from eleven open-weight agents (2 to 14 billion parameters) on two benchmarks, InjecAgent and AgentDojo [2]. (General explanation added by this site: an open-weight model usually means a model whose parameters are released publicly so that anyone can download and run it.)

Why it matters: with a label-blind language model acting as judge, most failed attacks turn out to be ignored (76–80%) rather than refused (17–18%) [2]. With identical model weights, changing only the system prompt shifts the attack success rate on InjecAgent from 0.2% to 43% [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a system prompt usually means instructions a developer writes for a model in advance to set its role and rules of behavior.) In 21.7% of AgentDojo episodes, the agent stayed safe but did not complete the user's task [2]. The authors therefore recommend that evaluations report how attacks fail and whether the user's task is completed, alongside the attack success rate [2].

**Item 3: Language model rewrites and predicted reading time.**

Background: in psycholinguistics, reading time is commonly used as an indirect measure of the effort of cognitive processing [3]. (General explanation added by this site: psycholinguistics usually refers to the study of the mental processes by which people understand and produce language.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-16, “The Cognitive Cost of LLM Writing: A Reading-Time Comparison with Human Text”, five authors, including Kamer Cekini, introduce RoBERTa-RT, a model trained on human eye-tracking data to predict word-level reading times [3]. (General explanation added by this site: eye tracking usually means using a device to record where a reader's gaze rests and for how long.) The authors build a paired dataset of 496 human-written articles and their language model rewrites, and use the model to compare their predicted reading times [3].

Why it matters: the language model rewrites tend to be shorter and have slightly lower predicted total reading times, but higher predicted reading times per word [3]. These differences are associated with systematic changes in language, including longer and less frequent words, greater lexical diversity and higher contextual predictability [3]. The authors suggest that language model writing may redistribute reading demands: there is less text to process, but the predicted processing effort for individual words goes up [3].
