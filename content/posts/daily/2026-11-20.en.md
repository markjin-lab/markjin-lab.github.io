Three research leads in this issue examine how to assess simulated users, queue inference requests, and judge search relevance with executable programs. The arXiv records list November 18, 2026 (UTC) as the submission date for all three preprints. This issue draws on the authors’ abstracts and metadata [1], [2], [3].

## Does the simulated user keep the original task?

Hengguang Zhou and colleagues deliberately altered simulated replays by changing goals, omitting constraints, or accepting broken work, among other changes. They then checked whether scoring methods penalized those failures. Under the same detection criterion, methods from three earlier studies detected five to six of the ten deliberate changes. The suite proposed by the authors detected all ten [1].

The authors also evaluated six user simulators on 351 real multi-turn sessions. The abstract reports that aggregate interaction patterns can miss when a simulated user follows the wrong goal or overlooks a constraint in an individual session [1]. A follow-up would check the threshold used for each change and whether the suite can detect naturally occurring failures on real tasks.

## Where do requests wait when inference capacity runs out?

Benjamin Novotny and colleagues report that Meta’s traffic shaper refuses work early when the backend reaches its admission limit and holds excess work in priority queues. The dispatcher probes upward or backs off in response to refusals. In a concurrent production A/B comparison on the same traffic, the system served a classifier with 43% fewer H100 GPUs than its queued, fixed-rate predecessor. Lower-priority work waited longer at peaks during the processing of backlogged work [2].

The figure measures GPU count in that setting. A follow-up would check throughput, latency targets, and waiting times at each priority before assessing whether the configuration transfers to other tasks.

## Can an executable program judge search relevance?

Eugene Agichtein and colleagues propose using a language model to turn assessment guidelines into executable programs for individual search topics. The programs then label documents for relevance. The authors compared methods on seven TREC evaluation collections. According to the abstract, the base method agreed with trained assessors at the level of Claude Sonnet 5.5 judges on TREC DL 2019–2021 [3].

The program allows readers to inspect how the rules apply to particular documents. A follow-up would check the topics and documents in each collection, the assessors’ criteria, and how the rules perform with new topics or document types.
