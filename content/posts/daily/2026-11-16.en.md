Three research leads in this issue ask how to turn questions about model behavior into checkable evaluation questions. They concern the influence of information in a prompt, the timing of alarms for model failures, and errors in streaming speech dictation. All three papers were submitted to arXiv on 2026-11-12 (UTC). This issue introduces the questions in their abstracts without reporting experimental findings [1], [2], [3].

## Research lead one: which parts of a prompt influence the output

**Sketching Language Models for Context Attribution**, Christopher Liaw, Aranyak Mehta, Grigoris Velegkas and Felix Zhou, arXiv:2611.17313v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [1].

The abstract frames context attribution as asking how removing part of a prompt changes a language model’s output. It studies leave-one-out attribution with a fixed measurement function [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a prompt is text supplied to a model; leave-one-out analysis removes one part at a time and observes changes in a specified output measure.)

Our next reading questions concern the measure used to define output changes, the restrictions imposed by the stability assumption, and how the conditions for counting model calls relate to the actual experiments. These questions require the full text. We do not report complexity formulas, error guarantees or experimental scores, present an abstract’s theoretical claim as a proof we have checked, or recommend a particular attribution tool.

## Research lead two: when an alarm for model failure should be evaluated

**Detection Is Not Early Warning: Evaluating Token-Level Alarms on Language Model Failures**, Marco De Negri and colleagues, arXiv:2611.16803v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [2].

The abstract distinguishes questions about classifying a completed response, detecting a failure after it begins, and evaluating an alarm before that beginning. It considers failures with an annotated onset [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a token is a unit used by a model to process or generate text; an onset annotation specifies where a process under study begins so that event timings can be compared.)

Our follow-up reading questions concern how alarm timing is defined, how thresholds are set, how false alarms are counted, and whether different failure types require different onset annotations. We have not checked the complete evaluation procedure or experiments. We do not report which monitor warns earlier, by how much, or whether any monitor is ready for deployment. The distinction in the paper’s title names the research lead; it does not validate a particular system’s warning capability.

Short extract from the abstract:

> an evaluation protocol for any failure with an annotated onset
>
> Translation: a protocol for evaluating failures whose onset has been annotated.

## Research lead three: how to classify errors in speech dictation

**Beyond Word Error Rate: A Failure Taxonomy of Streaming Dictation ASR**, Harras Mansoor, Muhammad Wisal, Hassan Bhatti and Masood Shakir, arXiv:2611.16919v1, submitted 2026-11-12 (UTC) [3].

The abstract places its question in dictation, where audio is streamed and the resulting text is used in a message. It discusses examining recognition, output formatting, language handling and, for some self-hosted systems, stages before audio reaches the model [3]. (General explanation added by this site: speech recognition converts speech into text; streaming starts processing while audio is still arriving; word error rate commonly aggregates word substitutions, deletions and insertions rather than directly counting a user’s editing actions.)

Our next reading questions concern speakers and recording conditions, language selection, processing before audio enters the model, and how the categories relate to the burden of editing. This is a reading checklist, not a finding that a particular product has a particular fault. We have not checked the full text and do not repeat brand rankings, error rates, effects of deployment settings or code-availability claims from the abstract, or generalize a test condition to all languages and uses.

Short extract from the abstract:

> what matters is which mistakes the user has to fix
>
> Translation: the relevant issue is which errors the user must correct.
