BitDecoding-FP4 lets large language models decode directly from a 4-bit floating-point KV cache; serving Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B on NVIDIA B300, it delivers 2.4–3.0 times higher peak serving throughput than a BF16 KV cache, while staying within 0.5 points of BF16 KV accuracy with NVFP4 [1]. Another survey scanning 10,000 target IP addresses for each of seven protocols finds that, at the origin-server level, post-quantum algorithm support reaches 9.1% for HTTPS and 7.39% for LDAPS, and falls below 1% for all other protocols [2].

## Today’s news through the Unified Systems View

For what the Unified Systems View is, see the [About page](/en/about/). Both papers in this issue can be read on arXiv; neither entry states publication or review status [1], [2].

**Item 1: Can the KV cache, the biggest memory consumer in long-context serving, be stored and computed in 4-bit floating point without a noticeable accuracy loss?**

Background: The abstract says the key-value (KV) cache dominates memory capacity and bandwidth in long-context large language model serving; 4-bit microscaling formats (NVFP4, MXFP4) shrink the cache and run attention on FP4 Tensor Cores, but existing FP4 KV systems use vanilla quantization that causes severe accuracy drops, whereas accuracy-oriented 4-bit algorithms rely on vector codebooks whose lookup overhead degrades decoding throughput [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a KV cache keeps the key–value states of processed tokens for reuse in later generation steps; FP4 is a 4-bit floating-point number format.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-06, "BitDecoding-FP4: Native FP4 KV Cache Decoding via Algorithm-System Co-Design", six authors—Dayou Du, Woon Yee Ng, Guangming Sheng, Nick Lau, Jianyi Cheng and Luo Mai—present BitDecoding-FP4, an algorithm–system co-design that mitigates three error sources, including splitting queries into two FP4 tensors to cut query logit error from 60% to 1%, and removes the softmax bottleneck at the system level with a transposed softmax with fused probability quantization, among other techniques [1].

Why it matters: Across dense and MoE models with GQA and MLA, BitDecoding-FP4 stays within 0.5 points of BF16 KV accuracy with NVFP4 (1 point with MXFP4) and tracks BF16 KV in RL training rollouts [1]. In end-to-end serving on NVIDIA B300 with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, it delivers 2.4–3.0 times higher peak serving throughput than BF16 KV [1].

{{< industry-link item="1" industry="ai" side="cost" inference="higher peak throughput matches a lower actual serving cost per token" >}}The source reports the peak serving throughput multiple over a BF16 KV cache [1], which maps to compute cost for long-context inference serving in this industry.{{< /industry-link >}}

Where it sticks: The throughput results come from two Qwen3 models on NVIDIA B300, and with MXFP4 accuracy stays within 1 point of BF16, a looser bound than the 0.5 points with NVFP4 [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other GPUs or models, can decode from a native FP4 KV cache with accuracy within about 1 point of BF16 while getting more than twice the peak serving throughput.

**Item 2: Have real-world servers adopted post-quantum cryptography to guard against future quantum computers breaking encryption?**

Background: The abstract says major CDNs and cloud providers are migrating to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) in anticipation of Q-day security concerns, yet while existing literature benchmarks PQC performance, empirical research evaluating real-world adoption data remains scarce [2]. (General explanation added by this site: post-quantum cryptography means cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks by quantum computers; Q-day usually means the day quantum computers can break today’s public-key encryption.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-06, "Empirical Assessment of Post-Quantum Cryptography Adoption Status in TLS", four authors—Md Ariful Haque, Sander Vinberg, Ben Edwards and João Cruz—assess PQC deployment across seven critical protocols (HTTPS, LDAPS, SMTPS, IMAP-SSL, POP3-SSL, TELNETS and RDP), using 10,000 target IP addresses per protocol [2].

Why it matters: At the origin-server level, PQC algorithm support reaches 9.1% for HTTPS and 7.39% for LDAPS, while falling below 1% for all other evaluated protocols; at the entity level, support is 11% for HTTPS and 5.4% for LDAPS, with fewer than 5% of entities supporting PQC on hosted servers for the remaining protocols [2]. The authors also correlate these results with organizational digital and financial footprint and industry sectors, and compare PQC adoption in Cloudflare-hosted domains against baseline Cloudflare datasets [2].

Where it sticks: Each protocol is sampled with 10,000 target IP addresses, and the abstract gives no specific figures for the comparisons with sectors or organization size [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, rescanning with larger samples or at later dates, find post-quantum support among HTTPS origin servers clearly above about one in ten, and whether email and other protocols begin to catch up.
