Three research leads examine whether models notice their own reasoning errors, how flood forecasts represent uncertainty, and how television captioning catches quiet speech. The three preprints were first submitted to arXiv on November 23, 2026 (UTC). This issue draws on metadata and the authors’ abstracts.

## Models respond to externally supplied doubt, while their own confidence barely distinguishes errors

Across 15,200 math benchmark attempts by four open-weight models, Daniel Theron isolated 2,017 reasoning errors within the respective models’ capabilities. At the same position in a chain of reasoning, the models’ stated confidence distinguished erroneous from sound steps with an AUROC of 0.500–0.561, close to the chance value of 0.5. Inserting a 10% confidence tag at an error increased the downstream correction rate by 3.7–22.5 percentage points across the four models relative to a format-matched 95% tag. When low confidence was inserted at an already correct step, the solve rate fell by up to 22.8 percentage points. [Author’s abstract · arXiv:2611.31228v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31228v1)

The experiment measures error detection and response to a correction cue separately. A next test would compare self-reported confidence with an external verifier on the same tasks, tracking both missed errors and needless changes to correct steps. The abstract reports results from math benchmarks. Performance on other tasks remains open. [Author’s abstract · arXiv:2611.31228v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31228v1)

## Lower average error can accompany a flood ensemble that is too narrow

Omar Sallam and colleagues trained a diffusion model on shallow-water simulations to estimate four days of water depths on a 30-metre grid in the Des Plaines River watershed in Illinois. Each forecast used 20 members drawn from the same model. In the study, the ensemble mean’s four-day mean root-mean-square water-depth error was 0.170 metres without a coarse hydrodynamic input, 0.054 metres with a 100-metre simulation and 0.048 metres with a 150-metre simulation. The authors also report spread-to-error ratios of 0.35–0.73, indicating that the ensemble did not span enough variation relative to its forecast errors. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.31212v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31212v1)

The 150-metre result shows that a finer input grid did not guarantee lower water-depth error in this study. Average error and probability calibration merit separate measurements. A next test would use independent flood events and observed water depths to measure both average error and the coverage of forecast intervals. The abstract reports the simulation comparison. Accuracy against field measurements remains to be checked. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.31212v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31212v1)

## A captioning pipeline rechecks rejected audio and cuts editing time in its sample

Aditya Mishra and colleagues describe two failures of single-pass voice activity detection: it can miss quiet speech, and it can forward near-silent audio that leads to invented or repeated text. Their CAST pipeline revisits rejected audio with semantic and acoustic checks and also incorporates segmentation, language identification and targeted transcription repair. Against a WhisperX pipeline on 100 in-house assets totalling 60.46 hours, the authors report that normalized word error fell from 6.99% to 5.36%. Subtitle editing time fell from 254.2 to 221.9 minutes per hour of source material. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.31055v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31055v1)

Those figures describe the combined pipeline. Comparing its components separately could establish how much of the gain came from rechecking quiet audio. The authors say CAST entered the production speech-to-text stage of their captioning workflow in late August 2026. Editing time at full scale remains to be checked. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.31055v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31055v1)
