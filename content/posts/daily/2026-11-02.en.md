A study had six frontier models act as research agents and propose 415 minimum viable experiments for seven recent NLP papers; model auditors judged 194 of them sound, valuable and new, but only 73 were also judged beyond the seed paper and non-obvious [1]. Another paper presents an AI-assisted proof evaluation pipeline that, compared with conventional one-shot LLM judgment, cuts false-positive verdicts on natural language proofs from 23% to 6% [2].

## Today’s news through the Unified Systems View

**Item 1: Before results are in, can research agents judge which experiments are worth running?**

Background: The abstract defines taste as the judgment of which experiments are worth running before their outcomes are known [1].

In a paper submitted to arXiv on 2026-10-29 (UTC), "rTaste: Do Research Agents Have Taste? Evaluating Early Stage Research Judgment", the author, Art Kanke, gives six frontier models seven recent NLP papers; each model, working as a research agent alone or as a team, proposes minimum viable experiments, forecasts whether each will pass a threshold it sets and ranks them, after which a separate executor attempts every experiment, including those ranked last, and three model auditors judge the results [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a minimum viable experiment is the smallest experiment designed to test an idea.)

Why it matters: Of 415 minimum viable experiments, 194 are judged sound, valuable and new, but fewer than half of these (73) are also judged beyond the seed paper and non-obvious [1]. The agents’ first choices match the auditors’ most valued experiment more often than a random pick but are not shown to improve on choosing the experiment written first; their forecasts beat a base rate but are not measurably more accurate than those of a fresh instance of the same model given only the paper and registrations; and teams cost more without a clear overall improvement in proposal quality [1].

Where it sticks: The results are judged by three model auditors [1]. The abstract says the agents produce many worthwhile experiments, but the evidence that their rankings improve the choice of which to run remains limited [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, using human researchers as judges, again see what this study found—only 73 of 415 agent-proposed experiments judged sound, valuable and new as well as beyond the seed paper and non-obvious—and whether agent rankings beat simply picking the first one written.

**Item 2: As AI takes part in more mathematical proofs, can judging those proofs at scale itself be made reliable?**

Background: The abstract says that as AI’s mathematical capabilities rapidly improve, meaningful benchmarks are shifting from university-level problems and math olympiads to research-level mathematics; the Ramanujan Challenge is a benchmark of research-level problems in number theory, including unpublished results and open conjectures, presenting formulas for mathematical constants whose correctness can be checked numerically while their proofs may require non-obvious mathematics [2].

In a paper submitted to arXiv on 2026-10-29 (UTC), "Ramanujan Challenge Results: AI-Assisted Evaluation Pipeline for Multi-Format Proofs", 11 authors (first listed: Alan Sorani, Ashvni Narayanan and Michael Shalyt) analyze the Challenge submissions, measuring the relative success of different AI models, computer algebra systems (CAS) and formal tools, and introduce an AI-assisted evaluation pipeline that assesses the semantic and logical correctness of natural language proofs, CAS code and Lean formalizations [2]. (General explanation added by this site: Lean is a formal proof language whose proofs can be checked by a computer.)

Why it matters: Compared with conventional one-shot LLM judgment, the pipeline reduces false-positive verdicts on natural language proofs from 23% to 6% and improves judgment accuracy on supporting code from 46% to 58% [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a false-positive verdict here means judging an incorrect proof to be correct.) The abstract also reports correct submissions that are conceptually distinct from the official solutions, and a proof of one of the published open conjectures [2].

Where it sticks: Judgment accuracy on supporting code stands at 58% after the improvement [2]. The abstract does not give specific success rates for the AI models, CAS and formal tools [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, in other areas of mathematics or in larger-scale proof review, can likewise bring false-positive verdicts on natural language proofs below one in ten with a multi-step AI-assisted pipeline.
