**Item 1: Do a few headline breakthroughs show what large language models can really do with research-level mathematical problems?**

Background: The abstract says the last year has seen a shift in AI-assisted mathematics, with frontier language models producing proofs of and counterexamples to long-standing open conjectures, but that studying only the current set of major breakthroughs gives an incomplete picture of large language model capabilities and of the computational resources needed to achieve reported results [1].

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-29, "Beyond Selected Breakthroughs: Evaluating Language Models on Randomly Sampled Mathematical Conjectures", four authors—Helen Jenne, Davis Brown, Jesse He and Henry Kvinge—introduce OpenConjecture, a dataset of conjectures from mathematics papers published on arXiv since February 2026, and apply frontier GPT models to a random sample of 200 of them over three passes, using a reproducible protocol without problem-specific mathematical guidance [1].

Why it matters: The models tested produce candidate solutions for roughly 60% of the conjectures, with counterexamples about 1.3 times more common than proofs [1]. Rather than attempt expert verification of these candidate solutions, the authors analyze the types of problems for which the models produce solutions and the characteristics of those solutions, estimating novelty, assessing how they cite and relate to existing literature, and checking whether a proposed resolution relies on an error in or underspecification of the conjecture [1].

Where it sticks: These are candidate solutions without expert verification [1]. The abstract says the models sometimes propose resolutions outside the authors’ intent (for example, proposing the trivial group as a counterexample), that a number of proofs follow closely from existing results, but that some resolutions exhibit at least moderate novelty [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, verifying model answers to randomly sampled open conjectures with experts, find what share of the candidate solutions hold up, and whether counterexamples still outnumber proofs.

**Item 2: Can inverter-level data make photovoltaic power forecasts for the next few tens of minutes more accurate?**

Background: The work targets 10- to 110-minute-ahead probabilistic photovoltaic power forecasting [2]. (General explanation added by this site: an inverter converts the direct current from solar panels into alternating current; a probabilistic forecast gives a range of possible values with their probabilities rather than a single number.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-29, "IMPACT: Inverter-Informed Multimodal Learning for Photovoltaic Generation Forecasting", six authors—Anushka Katiyar, Cong Feng, Jing Wang, Tristan Liu, Simon Julien and Zachary Jacobs—develop a physics-informed multimodal deep learning framework with four branches that separately encode plant-level temporal history, inverter-level spatial cloud dynamics through a graph attention encoder, known-future solar geometry and the current meteorological state, fused through gated cross-attention; the decoder anchors its prediction to a smart persistence baseline and learns a correction, and the resulting quantile forecasts are calibrated online [2].

Why it matters: The model outperforms all 14 baselines, achieving a forecast skill score (FSS) of 0.2950, 0.099 above the next-best model [2]. A branch-composition ablation shows that every branch contributes positively, and the model retains substantial skill in overcast and variable sky conditions (FSS 0.198 and 0.255) [2]. (General explanation added by this site: a forecast skill score measures how much a forecast improves on a reference forecast; higher is better.)

Where it sticks: The abstract does not state how many plants were tested or where, and reports no economic effect of the forecast improvement [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, at other photovoltaic plants or in other climates, can keep positive skill in overcast and variable weather with inverter-level short-term probabilistic forecasts.
