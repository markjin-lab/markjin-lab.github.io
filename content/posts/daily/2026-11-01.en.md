**Item 1: For long-horizon agents, can self-managing working memory finish hard tasks with far fewer input tokens?**

Background: LLM-powered agents often stall on long-horizon tasks when working memory fills with expanding, irrelevant information that dilutes attention and weakens planning; a way for the agent to tidy its own context is still needed [1]. (General explanation added by this site: working memory usually means the task state and intermediate facts an agent keeps while solving step by step; context usually means the full prompt text visible to the model in one inference pass.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-29, "Don’t Lose the Thread: Empowering Long-Horizon LLM Agents with Cognitive Resource Self-Allocation" (Accepted at COLM 2026), five authors—Zheng Zhu, Xiaozhuan Liang, Xi Chen, Bei Yu and Jiaya Jia—introduce Cognitive Resource Self-Allocation (CORAL), an agent-callable working-memory toolset that checkpoints progress and verified facts, then resets cluttered context while keeping only the checkpoint so reasoning can resume from a clean state [1].

Why it matters: on the GAIA benchmark, CORAL with an 8B model achieves 42.8% average accuracy, with particularly strong gains on the most challenging long-horizon tasks (Level 2 and Level 3) [1]. These gains come at substantially lower inference cost: CORAL consumes 6–7× fewer input tokens than a ReAct agent [1]. It also generalizes to multi-hop Knowledge QA and raises a strong proprietary agent by 8.0 points on the harder BrowseComp [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other long-horizon setups or other model scales, still find that checkpoint-style working-memory self-allocation holds or lifts hard-task accuracy while cutting input tokens to about one-sixth to one-seventh of a ReAct agent.

**Item 2: When many items share one prompt to save cost, do verdicts move with the batch?**

Background: to save cost, practitioners often ask a language model to grade many independent items in one prompt, assuming verdicts are separable—that a verdict does not depend on other items in the prompt or on position [2]. (General explanation added by this site: in-context batching usually means placing several items in one prompt for a joint grading pass; separable usually means a single verdict does not change with other items or their order.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-29, "Judged in Company: In-Context Batching Makes LLM Verdicts Depend on Other Items", two authors—Peng Xie and Amr Alanwar—test whether packing the same 2,000 items into random batches of 25 keeps decisions stable across three judgement tasks and five open-weight models with 4–9B parameters [2].

Why it matters: assigning the same 2,000 items to different random batches of 25 changes 11–22% of decisions on harm and grading, 6.5–13.3 points more than renaming item keys or rewording the instruction, while AUROC against human labels differs from single-item grading only by −0.033 to +0.010 [2]. For four models with 14–32B parameters, 6–15% of decisions still change [2]. Even the majority of fifteen random batches disagrees with single-item grading on 8.4–17.8% of decisions [2]. The authors recommend grading ambiguous items one per prompt and recovering most of the cost with a cached prefix [2].

Where it sticks: the abstract reports flip rates, AUROC gaps and residual majority-vote flips, without a full score table or a dollar cost gap [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other judgement tasks or other model families, still find that packing independent items into one prompt flips decisions far more than key renaming or instruction rewording, and that majority votes still struggle to match single-item grading.

**Item 3: Under latency targets, can speculative decoding’s energy saving turn into extra energy at large batch or low acceptance?**

Background: LLM serving is limited by facility power; energy per token sets how many tokens a datacenter can serve, while a latency target bounds how quickly users receive them. Speculative decoding drafts future tokens and verifies them in one forward pass, but every drafted token must be verified whether accepted or not, so the energy saved depends on acceptance [3]. (General explanation added by this site: speculative decoding usually means a smaller model drafts several next tokens and a target model verifies them in one pass, accepting or rolling back; acceptance rate usually means the share of drafted tokens the target keeps.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-29, "Only Accepted Tokens Count: An Energy Accounting of Speculative Decoding Under Latency Targets", five authors—Bole Ma, Ayesha Afzal, Jan Eitzinger, Harald Koestler and Gerhard Wellein—measure speculative decoding under production serving conditions (SGLang, CUDA graphs enabled) on H100 and H200 GPUs, at batch sizes up to 64 across 5 corpora [3].

Why it matters: on dense models, verification, not drafting, dominates the energy cost, and the saving shrinks as the batch grows because batching has already shared the weight read that speculation would divide among accepted tokens [3]. At large batches, block drafters can push verification into the compute-bound regime so the saving becomes a loss, and a low-acceptance drafter raises energy per token [3]. Meeting a tighter latency target with speculation then costs additional energy; larger batches already lower energy per token without speculation, and speculation adds to that saving only when acceptance is high [3].

Where it sticks: the abstract reports directional findings on verification, batch size and acceptance, without a single percentage summary table or a dollar electricity gap [3].
