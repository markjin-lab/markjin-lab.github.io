Three studies test how models estimate material properties from drop videos, recognise objects in unusual company, and carry out voice tasks with several people. All three preprints were first submitted on 25 November 2026 (UTC), more than 48 hours before this collection was frozen. This issue revisits them as research leads and draws on arXiv metadata and author abstracts.

## The best input for estimating material properties in simulation ranked last on filmed drops

Ziyue Peng and colleagues use a single filmed drop to estimate a deformable object’s stiffness and, when the impact causes permanent deformation, its yield stress. Both quantities are normalised by density. The authors report that short motion tracks lifted into three dimensions were the most accurate input in simulated validation but ranked last on filmed drops. Using LiDAR depth was among the controls that failed to remove that difference. On the authors’ filmed-drop benchmark, PhysReader, which uses the video and two-dimensional motion tracks, came closer to the material reference values than the other inputs or baselines. The one closer result came from optimising each video separately, starting from PhysReader’s prediction. The benchmark dropped 16 everyday objects five times each. Reference values were fitted jointly to each object’s drops and checked against tensile tests. [Author abstract · arXiv:2611.34894v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34894v1)

The authors suggest that two-dimensional tracks are measured more consistently across simulation and filmed drops. A follow-up could hold the model and material reference values fixed, vary camera angle, background and lighting, and compare errors for two- and three-dimensional inputs by material and filming condition. The reported results cover the controlled drop experiment. Performance on other objects and under other filming conditions remains open.

## Visual recognition still suffers when objects appear in unusual company

Siddhartha Gairola and colleagues generated photorealistic images with controlled layouts to see how recognition changed when objects appeared with common or unusual neighbours. The authors report performance gaps of 5 to 11 percentage points when objects appeared outside their usual company across five model families and three recognition tasks. Sampling more unusual pairings during training narrowed the gap on natural-image COCO splits by up to 1.6 percentage points. The first range describes the reported bias. The second figure is the largest reduction reported for the training change on those natural-image splits. [Author abstract · arXiv:2611.34881v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34881v1)

The experiment makes co-occurrence a condition that can be tested separately. One possibility to check is that some unusual pairings also make targets harder to see, for example by obscuring them. A follow-up on real photographs could match target size, visible area and position, then report the gap between common and unusual pairings separately for each model and task.

## A shared voice benchmark requires meeting everyone’s goals and privacy boundaries

Qihao Lin and colleagues tested voice assistants with simulated participants who reacted to what the assistant said and to tool outcomes. Each participant had their own goals, knowledge and permissions. The researchers evaluated 13 voice assistants on 800 cases, including 408 with tools and 392 without. Joint task success required a session to satisfy all user goals, respect privacy boundaries and reach the required end states. The authors report that, in controlled comparisons, joint task success declined as groups grew larger even among capable systems. [Author abstract · arXiv:2611.34920v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34920v1)

Shared conversations may make it harder to track who made a request and which exchange a follow-up belongs to. A trial with human participants could hold tasks, permissions and acoustics fixed, vary group size and follow-up frequency separately, and record both speaker attribution errors and joint task success. The abstract reports results from the simulated benchmark.
