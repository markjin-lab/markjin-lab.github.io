Three studies examine how to test chip designs, retest vision models after a repair, and measure how chatbot answer delivery affects votes. The three preprints were first submitted to arXiv on November 24, 2026 (UTC). This issue draws on metadata and the authors’ abstracts.

## A chip-design benchmark retests accepted designs on new sequences

Guangyuan Wang and colleagues use AIChipBench to test whether coding agents can implement language model decoders. Each time a decoder emits a token, it stores state for the next step. An incorrect state update may affect the output only several steps later. In all 12 matched agent and task combinations, feedback identifying the first state mismatch raised design acceptance by 25.0 to 46.9 percentage points compared with output-only feedback. When the authors retested the designs on new token sequences and conditions, 45 of 69 previously accepted designs failed within the 32 steps required by their tests. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.33093v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33093v1)

The benchmark checks state at each step and retests designs under new conditions. A next acceptance test could log the first output and state errors separately and reserve sequences that developers did not use. The abstract reports benchmark results. Performance on physical chips remains open.

## A vision model repairs held-out failures but loses accuracy on a separate set of negation questions

Ang Qi and Yue Wang study repairs to the vision-language model Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. In a human audit of 100 answers flagged as failures on questions generated from COCO and GQA, they found 37 incorrect labels. After verification and fine-tuning, their method corrected 82.8% of the held-out failures, compared with 10.0% for the base model. On a separate set of independently labelled question pairs that were not selected for failure, overall accuracy rose from 87.4% to 92.4%, while accuracy on negation questions fell from 88.7% to 79.7%. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.32982v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32982v1)

The repaired failures and the decline on negation questions came from different evaluation sets. A next test could keep an independently labelled sample intact and report results separately by spatial relation, negation and whether an object is absent. That would show where accuracy slips as the overall score rises. The figures apply to the model and data reported in the abstract.

## The same chatbot text drew different win rates depending on how it appeared

Zihan Chen and colleagues studied compar:IA, a French government chatbot comparison platform. When a prompt recurred, the platform randomly either streamed a fresh answer or immediately replayed a stored answer from the same model. Tracking the same stored text, the authors report that it won 57% of decisive blind votes when streamed and 29% when replayed. Each percentage uses decisive votes under that delivery condition as its denominator. In another analysis of 324,029 conversations on the platform, the authors found no detectable net voting advantage for slower streamed answers, but a slower first exchange was associated with fewer follow-up questions. [Authors’ abstract · arXiv:2611.33050v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33050v1)

Delivery can affect comparison votes, and follow-up questions capture a different part of the experience. A next test could keep the answer and waiting time fixed, randomly vary whether the text appears progressively or all at once, and track votes and follow-up questions separately.
