The main story today: A paper describes training a Brazilian Portuguese model to refuse harmful requests while still helping with ordinary ones: three attempts to train it from “better/worse” answers failed to reverse its compliance with harmful prompts, and this version's improvement came from later rounds of targeted safety examples [1]. Three other papers examine whether a model’s stated certainty can be trusted when it finds names and other items in long documents [2], why changed action names can hurt a task-performing model [3], and how to test the software that runs such a model [4].

**Item 1: Safety alignment should also record what failed.** Background: safety alignment means getting a model to refuse harmful requests more often while still helping with ordinary work; common methods include supervised fine-tuning (SFT, further training on written demonstration dialogues) and direct preference optimization (DPO, adjusting the model from “better/worse” answer pairs) [1]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-14, “Manacá-1B-Instruct-2: Reproducible Safety Alignment for a Native Brazilian-Portuguese LLM. Negative DPO Results, a Controlled Composition Scan, and a Silent Failure Mode of the LLM Judge”, three authors, including Bruno Leonardo Santos Menezes, describe the second-generation instruction alignment of Manacá-1B (about 1.72B parameters, trained from scratch for Brazilian Portuguese), with an SFT corpus of about 291,223 examples [1]. Why it matters: the authors report three DPO attempts across two generations—two from the previous version and one from this version—and none stopped the model from complying with harmful prompts [1]. The case in which a training measure reached about 1.0 without changing free responses came from the previous version; this version’s safety improvement came from three later rounds of dedicated safety training [1]. They trained each data mix only once on this model. As the share of general training examples rose from 10% to 50%, refusals of legitimate requests fell, then rose again at 60%. Each setting used 16 safety prompts and 34 prompts testing unwarranted refusal, so the proposed 40%–50% trade-off range is exploratory [1]. On the same items against four external Brazilian-Portuguese models, with confidence intervals and paired tests, the lower bound of this model's safety interval (94.1%) still sits above the upper bound of all four baselines [1]. They also report that on two of six safety items in MT-Bench-PT, the model judge categorically refuses to assign a score no matter the response, silently reducing the effective N (the number of items that actually get scored) for that category in every prior report of the project [1]. What changed: judging whether an alignment run “worked” cannot rest only on the final safety score; reports also need to say which methods were tried and failed, and whether the judge refused to score [1]. Where it is stuck: the findings are tied to a small Brazilian-Portuguese model and this project's own evaluation items; extending them to other languages and larger models still needs new evidence [1]. The link to item 2 is that item 1 warns that judges can refuse to score, while item 2 warns that stated confidence can rank outputs without verifying them; both call for writing silence and misleading signals into method notes [1], [2]. What to watch next: watch whether, by the end of 2026, a public safety-alignment technical report that posts final scores also lists failed preference-optimization attempts and states whether a model judge refused to score some safety items [1].

**Item 2: For entity extraction, stated confidence can only set review order.** Background: entity extraction asks a model to mark people, vulnerability identifiers, and other objects in a full document; stated confidence is the model saying in words how sure it is [2]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-14, “From Unreliable to Usable: Decomposing Failure Modes and Confidence Signals in LLM Entity Extraction - Evidence from Cyber Threat Intelligence”, Jiahong Qian runs a preregistered, frozen evaluation of two low-cost new-generation models (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash) and a flagship-tier control (GPT-6.1-Sol) on a public corpus of 354 incident campaigns (315 unique reports, 630 model–report evaluations) [2]. Why it matters: micro precision (the share of extracted items that are correct, pooled across all reports) is about 0.03–0.34 and verbalized calibration error (ECE, the gap between stated confidence and actual correctness; lower is better) about 0.53–0.94 across both splits, and the gap does not close at the flagship tier [2]. The dominant failure is not invention from nothing: the paper checks whether each extracted item actually appears in the report (a mention census), annotates a sample of candidates and weights the results back to the full set; of the estimated false positives, about 65.6% are background mentions (items that appear in the text but in an unrelated context) and about 19.9% are gold-scope gaps (relevant to the incident but outside the primary items listed in the answer key), with only about 1.2% unsupported candidates [2]. In the paper’s test set, ranking answers by how often the model repeats them improved the accuracy of the shortlist for human review. Yet in sentence-change tests covering 45 targets, one sentence assigning a fact to the wrong source reversed about 95.6%–100% of high-confidence judgments; even a sentence explicitly saying a vulnerability was unrelated sometimes led the model to attach it to the incident, in up to about 26.7% of cases [2]. The authors state a deployment boundary: confidence tiering may set review priorities, never truth [2].

**Item 3: Renaming an action can make an agent drop points.** Background: an agent chooses the next step from an interface (the names and notation of callable actions); renaming or rewriting those labels without changing the task can still hurt performance [3]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-14, “One Task, Many Interfaces: Simple Cross-Interface Consistency Training Improves LLM Agent Robustness”, Artin Tajdini, Manpreet Kaur and Peng Qi propose Cross-Interface Policy Regularization (CIPR): replace action names with uninformative identifiers, present each training sample under two interfaces whose equivalent actions are identified by construction, and penalize the difference between the action distributions under the two interfaces so the policy stays consistent [3]. On the same 2,501 single-turn tool-calling items, standard post-training on the same base model scored 40.6% on average across three interface changes withheld from training, versus 56.1% with the paper’s method; accuracy on the unchanged interface was 73.3% and 74.9%, respectively [3]. CIPR improves robustness in both supervised and reinforcement-learning post-training [3].

**Item 4: Score the agent's runtime harness on its own.** Background: a runtime harness is the engineering layer around the model that decides which tools may run, how state advances, and when the loop ends; most benchmarks measure task success under a fixed harness and rarely score harness writing itself [4]. In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-10-14, “Doc2Harness: Benchmarking LLMs on Long-Horizon Harness Engineering of Coding Agents”, eleven authors, including Yuyao Ge, propose Doc2Harness, a test of whether a model can build the software that runs a coding agent. First, the model under test works inside a fixed builder environment and, using a guide compiled from twelve existing agent runtimes plus a template that lists required interfaces without implementing them (a contract skeleton), builds a candidate program in an isolated container. Then a scripted stand-in for the model drives that frozen program in separate containers; the test checks requests to the model, files on disk, the process exit code, and command output, with extra checks (positive controls) so a case is not counted as passed when the feature under test never actually ran [4]. Across nineteen frontier models, each with three independent builds on 328 test cases, the highest pass rate is about 53.05% (GPT-6 Astra) and the lowest about 27.44%, a gap of roughly twenty-six percentage points; even the strongest model fails nearly half the cases, and failures often cluster around how cross-cutting functionality is split across modules [4].

## AI safety

**1. Manacá-1B-Instruct-2: Reproducible Safety Alignment for a Native Brazilian-Portuguese LLM. Negative DPO Results, a Controlled Composition Scan, and a Silent Failure Mode of the LLM Judge**

> We document three DPO attempts, all negative, including a case where the training reward margin reaches 1.0 while free generation does not change at all, and show that safety came from a dedicated safety-SFT (LLM-jp AnswerCarefully style), refined over three iterations until closing the safety-helpfulness frontier.

> The final model is compared against four external PT-BR models on the same items, with Wilson confidence intervals and paired McNemar tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction: the lower bound of its safety interval (94.1%) lies above the upper bound of all four baselines.

Primary source: arXiv:2610.20570, submitted 2026-10-14 (UTC) [1].
