A case study of DeepSeek-V4-Flash finds that directly reusing an old version’s KV cache after a model update gives median time-to-first-token speedups of 2.10–5.08 times on three longer-prefix QA benchmarks, but that the cache retains useful task information without faithfully reproducing the updated model’s behavior [1]. Another paper, SinkState, stores the recurrent states of hybrid models in adaptive low-rank form, cutting peak recurrent-state memory by up to 2.6 times and speeding up decoding by up to 1.58 times on four models from 4B to 120B parameters [2].

**Item 1: After a model is upgraded, can the KV cache computed by the old version still be used?**

Background: The abstract says reusing key–value (KV) caches across language model updates could avoid repeated computation of shared context, but changes in model weights raise a fundamental question about what these caches preserve [1]. (General explanation added by this site: a KV cache keeps the key–value states of processed tokens for reuse in later generation steps; time to first token is the time from submitting a request to generating its first token.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-10, "Can KV Cache Be Reused Across Model Updates? A DeepSeek-V4-Flash Case Study", three authors—Zhikun Tao, Fangyong Jin and Junping Zhao—investigate direct cache reuse across four model updates, with a detailed study of the update from DeepSeek-V4-Flash to Flash-0731, and introduce Functional Cache Divergence, which compares next-token distributions under reused and native caches [1].

Why it matters: With source caches already available, reuse achieves median time-to-first-token speedups of 2.10–5.08 times on three longer-prefix QA benchmarks [1]. Retrieval accuracy, however, varies substantially across updates, ranging from near-native performance to large losses [1].

Where it sticks: The abstract states that even agreement on the first generated token does not ensure agreement in answer content or stopping behavior, and that in single-token retrieval tasks compatibility degrades with increasing prefix length [1].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on version updates of other model families, can likewise get several-fold first-token speedups from direct KV cache reuse while keeping retrieval accuracy losses small.

**Item 2: Can the recurrent states that compress history in hybrid models be compressed further to speed up decoding?**

Background: The abstract says hybrid models combine softmax attention with linear attention, which compresses history into fixed-size recurrent states to balance model quality and inference efficiency [2]. The authors find that the queries, keys and values of linear layers concentrate in a stable, low-dimensional subspace, which they call the linear attention sink, so each state is effectively low-rank; yet existing serving systems keep these states dense, reading and rewriting them in full at every decoding step, so their memory traffic grows with the batch size and dominates the decoding cost [2]. (General explanation added by this site: low-rank means a matrix can be approximated with far less data.)

In a paper posted on arXiv on 2026-11-10, "SinkState: On-the-Fly SVD of Recurrent States for Efficient Hybrid LLM Serving", six authors—Haolin Jiang, Weihang Li, Zhongzhu Zhou, Jinwen Pan, Santosh Pandey and Hang Liu—present SinkState, which stores each recurrent state in an adaptive low-rank form throughout inference with buffered updates and a local SVD restricted to the top subspace, and consolidates the remaining components asynchronously on the CPU [2].

Why it matters: On four hybrid models with Mamba-2 and Gated DeltaNet (GDN) layers, from 4B to 120B parameters, SinkState reduces the peak memory of recurrent states by up to 2.6 times (1.9 times on average) and speeds up decoding by up to 1.58 times (1.25 times on average) over Mamba-SSM and FLA, while maintaining accuracy [2].

{{< industry-link item="2" industry="ai" side="cost" inference="lower memory use and faster decoding match a lower actual cost of inference serving" >}}The source reports the peak recurrent-state memory cut and decoding speedup over Mamba-SSM and FLA [2], which maps to memory cost for hybrid-model inference serving in this industry.{{< /industry-link >}}

Where it sticks: The results cover only hybrid models with Mamba-2 and GDN layers, and the abstract gives multiples over two existing systems without a full accuracy comparison [2].

What to watch next: this is our own point to watch, not a conclusion of the paper: whether other teams, on other linear-attention or hybrid architectures, also observe low-rank structure in recurrent states and use it to get around a twofold memory cut and decoding speedups of one-fifth or more.
