Three research leads in this issue examine where robot actions fail, how language models turn internal information into answers, and how to check outputs from ocean engineering simulations. The three preprints were submitted to arXiv on November 19, 2026 (UTC). This issue draws on the metadata and authors’ abstracts [1], [2], [3].

## Where robot actions fail

Yiyang Du and colleagues analyzed models that controlled robots directly across 12 manipulation benchmarks. Task success rates give the outcome of an entire attempt. The authors had the control model state its immediate intent for each action, then used a critic model to assess the result against that intent. They observed that models could sometimes recover from an action failure using feedback. Grasping and alignment remained bottlenecks, and recovery varied by failure type [1].

The authors validated the critic on a benchmark of 400 human-annotated actions. That figure counts actions in the critic’s validation benchmark. The abstract gives neither denominators nor recovery rates by failure type, so it cannot establish operational reliability. A next check would compare human annotations, action counts and recovery rates for each failure type, then test the evaluation method in new settings [1].

## How internal information becomes an answer

Jochen Laubrock compared six open-weight base models and their post-trained versions. On the task of judging whether German texts were written by a human or by AI, linear probes could distinguish the two classes from internal representations. The models’ direct answers distinguished them less well, and their average sensitivity fell after post-training. A linear probe is a simple classifier trained separately on internal representations [2].

The author reports the opposite change on a task that asks whether negated statements are true. The probes already identified truth with high accuracy, and post-training improved direct answers from several base models that had performed poorly on that task. The author says the German authorship corpora cannot be shared. The two experiments suggest that the effect of post-training on how models turn internal representations into answers may depend on the task. Testing probes and direct answers on new, publicly inspectable data and different prompts could check whether the German authorship result generalizes [2].

## What to check after a simulation script runs

In their OceanCoder study, Can Xue and colleagues evaluated automated modeling for ocean engineering with three levels of verification. They checked whether inputs met engineering conditions, whether the required analyses ran to completion, and whether the outputs were plausible in engineering terms. In the authors’ evaluation, the share of first generated results that passed (Pass@1) rose from 0% for the base model to 46.77% after supervised fine-tuning and 51.43% after reward optimization [3].

The authors say the benchmark covers out-of-distribution tasks and real engineering cases. The abstract gives only aggregate results. Pass rates and error ranges for individual engineering cases still need checking. To assess a practical simulation, an engineering team could compare the same case against an independent calculation or measurements and check the acceptance thresholds for its intended use [3].
