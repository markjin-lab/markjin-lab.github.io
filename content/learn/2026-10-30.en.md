## 01 The latest event: “stable” only becomes useful when unpacked

On 28 October the Ministry of Transport published a news brief: transport in the first three quarters was “generally stable”, with year-on-year growth in commercial freight, port cargo throughput and cross-regional passenger flows, and a positive rebound in September freight indicators. The formal statistical wording is in the 26 October government disclosure and its PDF. We take the 28 October brief as the event within 72 hours and check figures against the Comprehensive Planning Department release. This is a January–September operating brief, not a single-day port operations report. [1] [2] [3]

For a water-transport engineer, the easiest pair to confuse is waterway freight of 6.39 billion tonnes, up 2.2%, versus national port cargo throughput of 13.64 billion tonnes, up 0.7%. Both rose, but at different speeds, and they measure different objects. [2] [3]

## 02 A comparable trend: cumulative “stability” does not make September stand for Q4

Commercial freight in January–September was 43.04 billion tonnes, up 2.3%; September alone was up 1.3%. Port cargo throughput rose 0.7% over nine months and 2.6% in September. Singling out September’s rebound does not authorize extrapolating that month to the fourth quarter. Cumulative and monthly growth rates use different window lengths; subtracting them does not yield an “implied” rate. [2] [3]

## 03 One mechanism: freight volume and port throughput are different rulers

Waterway freight counts the weight of goods moved by commercial water transport. Port cargo throughput counts the weight of goods loaded or discharged at ports. The same cargo may bypass counted ports, or be handled at more than one port and counted more than once; river–sea links and transshipment keep the two series from matching one-for-one. Mechanism only: suppose an inland stretch moves 10 tonnes, of which 6 are handled at a hub port and 4 at a feeder berth outside the same port series—then the increments in “waterway freight” and “port throughput” need not be equal. These numbers illustrate a mechanism; they are not a Ministry breakdown.

Structure adds another cut: domestic port throughput fell 0.2% year on year in January–September, while foreign-trade throughput rose 2.9%; container throughput was 280 million TEU, up 4.5%. Container growth above total tonnage growth means boxes and bulk/breakbulk pull total tonnes differently. Container growth is not a substitute for whole-port tonnage, nor a freight-rate signal for one ship type or one trade lane. [2] [3]

## 04 Company and physical side: construction progress is not already-counted throughput

The same news brief notes progress at Shanghai Port’s third automated terminal—Xiaoyangshan North—with five automated quay cranes in place. SIPG Shengdong reported on 23 October that by 20 October the five automated quay cranes, 20 automated rail-mounted gantries and five shore-powered rubber-tyred gantries needed for opening had all been landed, with joint commissioning under way and a target of completing core-equipment production trials by late November ahead of a year-end opening. That is capacity under construction and equipment arrival, not throughput already in this period, and it cannot be used to attribute Shanghai’s or the national ports’ quarterly increment. [1] [4]

Transport fixed-asset investment in January–September was RMB 2.4 trillion, of which water transport accounted for RMB 157.1 billion. Investment is a flow that builds capacity; throughput is the result of using capacity. Lags are common; the two growth rates should not be matched one-to-one. [2] [3]

## 05 Counterevidence, feedback and what to watch next

Counterevidence sits in the same table: waterway passengers were 200 million, down 1.2%; domestic port throughput fell while foreign trade and containers rose. Overall “stability” does not rule out weaker passengers or softer domestic cargo. Calling September’s port rebound a “broad demand recovery” is not supported by the public release; it only supports that September’s year-on-year change for that indicator was positive and above the cumulative rate. [2] [3]

Conversely, if foreign-trade port throughput keeps outpacing domestic, coastal mainline and feeder box distribution may shift, then schedules and yard turns. If automated berths open on time, unit-shoreline productivity may rise and ease later capacity constraints. These are mechanisms to test, not established macro effects. Next, watch comparable monthly domestic/foreign and container series, hub-port operations and opening-node disclosures. For a water-transport engineer, separate freight tonnes, port tonnes, boxes and investment before judging heat or cold.
